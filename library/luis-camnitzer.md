# Luis Camnitzer

Uruguayan, born 1937 in Lübeck, Germany, raised in Uruguay, based in New York since the 1960s. Artist, teacher and writer, and the main voice arguing that Latin American conceptualism is its own tradition.

## Who
Camnitzer makes works out of language, labels and plain objects, and he writes about art as a form of education and politics. He was part of the New York Graphic Workshop in the 1960s (it published his Living Room) and is professor emeritus at the State University of New York. His book Conceptualism in Latin American Art: Didactics of Liberation (2007) argues that Latin American artists used conceptual means to respond to crisis, not mainly to attack the art object or the market as in New York. He sees himself as a Latin American artist exiled in the art world's capital.

## Key works
- This is a mirror. You are a written sentence., from 1966. The Centre Pompidou's version is dated 1966 to 1975: a wooden box with glass and a brass plate bearing only that sentence. The words name what the object is and also turn the reader into text. The often-cited end date of 1968 was not confirmed. [checked]
- Living Room (Living Comedor), 1968 (artist's book, MoMA) and 1969 (installation, per Reina Sofía). A room whose furniture and objects exist only as written labels on the walls and floor. [checked]
- Leftovers, 1970. The Reina Sofía marks it as the start of his openly political work. Commonly described as stacked boxes wrapped in stained gauze and stenciled with numbers; that description was not confirmed on a page read. [checked for title and year; verify description]
- Uruguayan Torture Series, plates dated 1983 (Blanton; Reina Sofía gives 1982). 35 photo-etchings, each pairing an ordinary-looking photograph with a short phrase, about torture under Uruguay's dictatorship (1973 to 1985). Nothing violent is shown; the viewer makes the link. [checked]
- Lección de Historia del Arte, Lesson No. 1, 2000, and El aula, 2005. Large installations shaped as lessons and classrooms. [checked]

## Core moves
- Language as object: a sentence takes the place of the thing it describes.
- Labeling the world: name an object instead of showing it, and let the viewer build the room.
- The gap between word and image: put a caption next to a photo that does not seem to match, so the mind fills the space.
- Didactics: art as a lesson that teaches people to think rather than a product to buy.
- Shared authorship: the viewer finishes the work, which Camnitzer treats as a political act.
- Restraint about violence: show the trace or the euphemism, never the act.

## Ideas worth keeping
Camnitzer argues that conceptualism is a strategy, not a style. In Latin America, where art institutions were weak and politics were urgent, the point was not to make art more pure but to make it useful: to wake people up and teach them to read the world. He traces this back to the 19th century teacher Simón Rodríguez and sees the Tupamaros guerrillas' staged actions as politics that crossed into art.

The torture prints show his method at its sharpest. The images are calm, the phrases almost poetic, and the horror only appears in the reader's head. The Blanton cites his hope for "a perceptual and political awakening" (Blanton Museum, Uruguayan Torture Series). The work refuses spectacle and trusts the viewer.

## What Mario could learn
- Write a single sentence that changes the status of the person reading it.
- Pair a mild image with a mild phrase whose combination is not mild at all.
- Frame a piece as a lesson or exercise and ask what it teaches that schools do not.
- Describe a space only through labels and let the reader furnish it.
- Do not reuse: "This is a mirror. You are a written sentence," rooms made of word labels, photo plus phrase sets about torture, gauze-wrapped numbered boxes, art history "lessons" in classroom form.
