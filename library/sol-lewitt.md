# Sol LeWitt

American, 1928 to 2007. Central figure of Minimal and Conceptual art in New York.

## Who
LeWitt wrote the two texts that gave Conceptual art its name and its rules of thumb. He argued that the idea is the work, and that once the plan is set, carrying it out is routine. His wall drawings exist as instructions that other people execute, so the same work can be made, erased and made again in many places. He was also generous to younger artists and swapped works with many of them.

## Key works
- Serial Project, I (ABCD), 1966. Enamelled open and closed square units laid out on a grid, combined in every variation of a simple rule, so viewers can work out the system from the result. [checked]
- Paragraphs on Conceptual Art, 1967. An essay in Artforum, volume 5, number 10, summer (June) 1967, pages 79 to 83. It opens by saying that in conceptual art the idea is the most important part of the work. [checked]
- Wall Drawing #1: Drawing Series II 14 (A & B), 1968. His first wall drawing, in black pencil, first installed in October 1968 at Paula Cooper Gallery, 96 Prince Street, in the gallery's first show, a benefit for the Student Mobilization Committee to End the War in Vietnam. It was priced "by hour", by the cost of installing it, and did not sell. [checked]
- Sentences on Conceptual Art, 1969. A numbered list of short statements. First published in January 1969 in the magazine 0 to 9, edited by Vito Acconci and Bernadette Mayer, then in May 1969 in the first issue of Art-Language. [checked]
- Incomplete Open Cubes, 1974. A project mapping all 122 ways a cube's frame can be incomplete yet still read as a cube in three dimensions, shown as sculptures, drawings and photographs. [checked]
- Xerox Book, 1968. LeWitt was one of seven artists in Seth Siegelaub's book exhibition, each given 25 pages. [checked]

## Core moves
- Instruction: the work is a set of directions that others carry out.
- System: a simple rule run through all its permutations.
- Transfer of authorship: drafters make the work; the caption credits them.
- The rule and its record: the plan, the result and the certificate travel together.
- Impermanence by design: walls are painted over and the work survives as text.
- The manifesto as work: numbered sentences that set terms for everyone.

## Ideas worth keeping
LeWitt took the romance out of making. If the idea is fixed in advance, then the hand that executes it matters less, and the work can be repeated anywhere by anyone who follows the text. That also makes the work strangely durable: a painted-over wall loses nothing, because the drawing lives in its instructions. His phrase for this was "The idea becomes a machine that makes the art" (Paragraphs on Conceptual Art, 1967).

His systems are not cold. Running a rule through every possible case often produces surprises the artist did not foresee, and the gap between a clear rule and an odd result is where the work becomes interesting. The Incomplete Open Cubes turn a dry question (how many ways can a cube be missing parts?) into a visual catalogue of near misses.

## What Mario could learn
- Write the rule first, then let it run to the end, including the awkward cases.
- Credit the people who carry out the instructions as part of the work.
- Price, sell or record a work in a way that shows what it really is (time, labour, text).
- Let a work be destroyed and remade without loss, because it lives in language.
- Do not reuse: the line "The idea becomes a machine that makes the art"; numbered wall drawings made from instructions by drafters; a list of 35 numbered sentences on art; the 122 incomplete cubes; an ABCD serial grid of open and closed cubes.
