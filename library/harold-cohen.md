# Harold Cohen

British-born, worked in the United States, 1928 to 2016. An established London painter who moved to the University of California, San Diego, in the late 1960s and spent over forty years writing AARON, one of the first art-making computer programs.

## Who
Cohen was a successful abstract painter in London (a Slade graduate of 1950) when he turned to computing. He wanted to know what drawing really is: what rules a person follows when marks start to look like images. His answer was to write those rules into a program, AARON, which he kept rewriting from the early 1970s until his death. AARON is a key ancestor of today's AI image tools, but it learned nothing from data. Everything it knew, Cohen had put into it by hand.

## Key works
- AARON, from about 1973. The program itself, which Cohen named in the early 1970s and developed for over four decades. It began in FORTRAN, moved to C, then to LISP. The Computer History Museum dates his work with AARON from 1973. [checked]
- Mazes, 1971 to 1972. Early computer drawings from the period just before AARON took its name; restored in 2023 for the Whitney show. [checked]
- Turtle drawings, late 1970s. A small robot "turtle" holding a pen drew AARON's images live in the gallery, as at SFMOMA in 1979; a Tate Gallery show followed in 1983. [checked]
- Untitled [Amsterdam Suite], 1978, and Untitled, 1982. Black and white AARON drawings, many later coloured by Cohen by hand. [checked]
- Painting by Aaron, 1995. From the period when Cohen built a painting machine (a robotic arm on a vacuum table) and the first AARON that chose its own colours. [checked]
- Harold Cohen: AARON, Whitney Museum of American Art, 3 February to 19 May 2024. A survey with plotters drawing live in the gallery. [checked]

## Core moves
- Knowledge as code: writing down an artist's tacit rules so a machine can follow them.
- The lifelong program: one piece of software as a single, decades-long work, revised like a diary.
- Live machine drawing: showing the act of drawing by the machine, not only its results.
- Hand finishing: machine-drawn lines coloured or completed by the human, mixing authorship.
- Studio assistant model: the machine as an apprentice, not a genius.
- Refusing to claim intelligence: being honest about what the program does not know.

## Ideas worth keeping
Cohen's project was a theory of drawing tested by building it. If you can make a machine produce images that people read as figures, plants or rooms, you have learned something about how people see. He was careful in his claims. He compared AARON to the assistants of Renaissance painters, said it was less creative than he was in writing it, and noted that it never improved itself. The Computer History Museum quotes him: "It's taken me 20 years to teach AARON to draw" (1989).

His work offers a contrast to today's systems. AARON's knowledge was small, explicit and authored line by line. Modern models are vast and learned from other people's images. Cohen's question, who is the author when a program draws, is still open, but his version keeps the author visible.

## What Mario could learn
- Make the rules visible. A work can be the written list of what a machine was told, next to what it made.
- Treat a long-running system as one artwork with versions, not as a source of separate pictures.
- Ask what a machine does not know and make that gap the subject.
- Split the labour openly: state which marks are machine and which are human.
- Do not reuse: the name AARON, robot turtles drawing on the floor, plotters drawing figures live in a gallery, hand-coloured machine line drawings of people and plants, the "teaching the machine to draw" framing as a title.
