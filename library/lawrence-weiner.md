# Lawrence Weiner

American, 1942 to 2021. Born in the Bronx, worked in New York and Amsterdam. Made language itself the material of sculpture.

## Who
Weiner made sculpture out of sentences. After 1968 his works were short phrases describing materials and actions, shown as lettering on walls, in books, on posters or in public places. His statement of intent said a work could be built by him, by someone else, or not at all, and that the owner decides. This split the work from any single object and gave the receiver a real role.

## Key works
- A Series of Stakes Set in the Ground at Regular Intervals to Form a Rectangle..., 1968. At Windham College, Vermont, an outdoor stake-and-string work was cut down by students who found it in the way. Weiner then kept the work as its written description, the turn toward language. [checked]
- Statements, 1968. His first book, published by Seth Siegelaub in December 1968, cheaply printed in 1,000 copies with no pictures. Each work is a typed phrase in the past tense. MACBA counts 25 works (12 general, 13 specific); Britannica says 24. [checked]
- One Standard Air Force Dye Marker Thrown into the Sea, 1968. One of the works published in Statements. [checked]
- Statement of intent, 1968. Three equal options: the artist may construct the work, it may be fabricated, it need not be built; the receiver decides its condition. Dated 1968 by Lisson Gallery and 1969 by UCCA. The January 5 to 31, 1969 catalogue included a statement by Weiner; that this was the first printing is likely but [verify]. [checked]
- THE RESIDUE OF A FLARE IGNITED UPON A BOUNDARY, 1969. Listed as "language + the materials referred to". In 1969 it was carried out as a flare lit at Amsterdam's city edge, and it can also exist as text on a card or wall. [checked]
- A BIT OF MATTER AND A LITTLE BIT MORE, 1976. A wall text work in MoMA's collection. [checked]
- IN DIRECT LINE WITH ANOTHER & THE NEXT, 2000. Nineteen manhole covers with text set into the streets of Lower Manhattan, commissioned by the Public Art Fund with Con Edison. [checked]

## Core moves
- Language as object: the phrase is the sculpture, not a caption for it.
- Optional making: the work exists whether or not anyone builds it.
- The receiver decides: ownership includes the power to realise or not.
- Past tense: the description reads as done, not as an order.
- Cheap circulation: books and posters that anyone can afford and carry.
- Public lettering: text set into everyday places (pavements, walls, covers).

## Ideas worth keeping
Weiner treated a sentence as a way to hold matter. A phrase like a residue of a flare on a boundary is specific enough to picture and open enough to happen in many places. Because the work is the language, a reader who imagines it has already received it. The physical version, if any, is one of several equal forms.

His statement of intent is also an ethics of ownership. It refuses to make the artist the only legitimate maker, and it hands the final decision to whoever receives the work. In its plainest line: "THE WORK NEED NOT BE BUILT" (Lisson Gallery).

## What Mario could learn
- Describe a material action so precisely that reading it is enough, then leave building optional.
- Choose verb tense on purpose: an order, a plan and a report are three different works.
- Give the receiver a real decision and say so in the work's terms.
- Put text where it will be met by chance (a street, a form, a receipt) rather than in a frame.
- Do not reuse: the three-part statement of intent (may construct, may be fabricated, need not be built); "the decision rests with the receiver"; dye markers thrown into the sea; flares on a boundary; stakes forming a rectangle; capitalised material phrases joined by "AND" or "&" on walls; text on manhole covers.
