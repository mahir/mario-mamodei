# Sophie Calle

French, born 1953. Works in Paris. Makes works of photographs and text from rules she sets for her own life: following, hiring herself out, collecting strangers' traces.

## Who
Calle turns everyday life into a game with strict rules, then reports the result with photographs and short texts. She has followed strangers, worked as a chambermaid to study guests' belongings, rebuilt a man's portrait from his lost address book, and handed her own heartbreak to more than a hundred women to interpret. She represented France at the 2007 Venice Biennale. She matters because she joined the rule-based methods of Conceptual art to autobiography, intimacy and narrative, and because her work keeps asking where curiosity turns into intrusion.

## Key works
- The Sleepers (Les Dormeurs), 1979. Over 1 to 9 April 1979, she invited 28 people, friends and strangers, to sleep in her bed in turns of about eight hours, and photographed them and took notes, asking about their sleep. [checked]
- Suite vénitienne, 1980. After meeting a man in Paris, she secretly followed him to Venice and tracked him through the city, in disguise, recording it in photographs and a diary. The Pompidou names it as one of the works that set out her method of self-made situations and rules. [checked for title and year; the details of the story are known, not confirmed on a page I read]
- The Hotel (L'Hôtel), 1981 (work dated 1981 to 1983). She took a job as a replacement chambermaid in a Venice hotel from 16 February to 6 March 1981 and photographed and described the belongings in the rooms she cleaned. Held by the Centre Pompidou. [checked]
- The Address Book, 1983. She found an address book in Paris, copied it, returned it anonymously, then contacted the people in it to build a portrait of its owner, publishing the results as a serial in the daily Libération over a month. [checked]
- Last Seen..., 1991. After six paintings were stolen from the Isabella Stewart Gardner Museum in Boston in 1990, she asked curators, guards and other staff to describe the missing works from memory, and showed their words beside photographs of empty spaces and bare hooks. [checked]
- Take Care of Yourself, 2007. A man ended their relationship by email, signing off "take care of yourself". She asked 107 women to read the letter through their professions (among them a crossword setter, an actress, a forensic psychiatrist, musicians) and showed all the responses in the French Pavilion in Venice. [checked]

## Core moves
- Self-set rule: a game imposed on her own days.
- Following: tracking a stranger as a form of attention.
- Inventory of traces: what people leave in rooms, books, beds.
- Portrait by absence: describing someone or something through what others say about it.
- Collective reading: one private text passed to many interpreters.
- Text plus photograph: the caption as half of the work.

## Ideas worth keeping
Calle asks whether you can know someone from the outside: from their luggage, their contacts, their sleep. The answer is always partly yes and finally no, and the gap between data and person is where her work lives. Her methods look like a detective's or a spy's, yet the tone is tender and sometimes funny. This raises a real ethical question: The Address Book is widely reported to have angered its owner, who had not agreed to be portrayed, and that tension is part of why the work is still discussed.

Last Seen is one of the clearest models of art made from absence: a lost painting survives only as people's unequal memories of it. Take Care of Yourself shows the opposite move: a private hurt dissolved by being read too many ways at once.

## What Mario could learn
- Set a rule for a fictional life, then write the report as if the rule were followed.
- Portray a missing thing only through others' memories of it.
- Hand one short text to many imagined readers with different trades and let their readings be the work.
- Keep the ethical edge visible: name the line between attention and intrusion.
- Do not reuse: strangers sleeping in the artist's bed, following a man to Venice, a chambermaid photographing rooms, a found address book, guards describing stolen paintings, a breakup email read by 107 women.
