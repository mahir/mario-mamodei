# Mel Bochner

American, 1940 to 2025. Born in Pittsburgh, worked in New York. Early Conceptual artist of measurement, documents and language.

## Who
Bochner helped start Conceptual art with an exhibition of photocopies, then made rooms into diagrams of their own size. From 1970 he wrote statements about language directly on walls, and in later decades he painted lists of synonyms that slide from polite to crude. His work keeps asking how measuring, naming and copying shape what we think we see. He died in New York on 12 February 2025.

## Key works
- Working Drawings and Other Visible Things on Paper Not Necessarily Meant to Be Viewed as Art, 1966. A Christmas show at the School of Visual Arts gallery: Bochner photocopied sketches, notes and plans by artists such as Sol LeWitt, Eva Hesse, Donald Judd and Robert Smithson and bound them in four binders. [checked]
- 36 Photographs and 12 Diagrams, 1966. An early work pairing photographs with diagrams, named by ArtReview among his first conceptual pieces. [checked]
- Measurement Room, 1969. First made for his solo show at Heiner Friedrich's gallery in Munich. Tape and numbers record the dimensions of the gallery walls on the walls themselves. Dia:Beacon realised its largest version in 2019. [checked]
- Language Is Not Transparent, 1970. A rough black painted field on the wall with dripping edges, and the words written over it in chalk. [checked]
- Thesaurus paintings, after 2000. Bright canvases headed by one loaded word, followed by synonyms and phrases that run from neutral to vulgar. [checked]
- The Joys of Yiddish, 2012. Yellow words on black, shown in the Jewish Museum's Strong Language exhibition, and a frieze version for Munich's Haus der Kunst in 2013. [checked]

## Core moves
- The copy as exhibition: photocopies replace originals and the binder is the show.
- Measurement: the room records its own size.
- Language as surface: words written on paint to show that words are also material.
- Inventory of synonyms: a word's meaning spread out until it turns.
- The working document: sketches and notes promoted to art status.
- Self-description: a space or a sentence that reports on itself.

## Ideas worth keeping
Bochner's binders asked a sly question: if a working drawing is shown as a photocopy, in a binder, on a plinth, is it art? The title answers "not necessarily", and that hedge is the point. Art historian Benjamin Buchloh called the show "probably the first truly conceptual exhibition" (as quoted in ArtReview). It also put the private paperwork of art making in public view.

His measurement works turn a room into its own diagram. You stand inside the numbers that describe where you stand. Language Is Not Transparent extends this: a sentence about language is written over messy paint, so you see words as a physical layer, not a window. The later synonym paintings show how meaning drifts as you move along a list.

## What Mario could learn
- Show the working papers of something (notes, plans, drafts) as the finished work, and hedge the claim in the title.
- Make a space or object record its own measurements, so viewer and description overlap.
- Write a statement about language in a form that makes the statement visibly true.
- Lay out a list of near-synonyms so the reader feels meaning shift step by step.
- Do not reuse: binders of photocopied drawings on plinths; "not necessarily meant to be viewed as art"; tape-and-number measurements of a gallery's walls; "Language Is Not Transparent"; thesaurus lists that slide into insults; "Blah Blah Blah".
