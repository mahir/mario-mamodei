# Hi-Red Center

Japanese collective, Tokyo, active 1963 to 1964 (Wikipedia says founded May 1963), by Jirō Takamatsu (1936 to 1998), Genpei Akasegawa (1937 to 2014) and Natsuyuki Nakanishi (born 1935; widely reported to have died in 2016, not confirmed here).

## Who
Hi-Red Center took its name from the first character of each founder's surname: taka (high), aka (red) and naka (center). Its members staged deadpan actions in trains, hotels and streets that copied the forms of public order (medical checks, cleaning campaigns) to mock them. They worked in the years of protest after the 1960 US-Japan security treaty and in the run-up to the 1964 Tokyo Olympics, when the state was eager to show a clean, modern city. They had close links to Fluxus in New York. Akasegawa's prosecution for printing model 1,000-yen notes turned the group's questions about art and the state into a real court case.

## Key works
- Yamanote Line Incident, 18 October 1962 (before the group was named). Nakanishi, Takamatsu and others rode the Yamanote loop line in Tokyo; Nakanishi, face painted white, held and licked one of his resin objects while others behaved oddly among ordinary passengers. [checked]
- Shelter Plan, January 1964, Imperial Hotel, Tokyo. Invited guests, including Yoko Ono and Nam June Paik, had their bodies measured and examined as if for custom-made nuclear fallout shelters. The month comes from Wikipedia; MoMA's text dates related objects to 1964. [checked]
- Be Clean! (Cleaning Event), October 1964, Ginza, Tokyo. Members, in white coats and masks in the well-known photos, scrubbed a small patch of public pavement with great care during the Olympics, mocking the official clean-up campaign. Its official title was roughly "Be Clean! and Campaign to Promote Cleanliness and Order in the Metropolitan Area." Wikipedia gives 16 October 1964. [checked for month and year; exact day verify]
- Akasegawa, Model 1,000-yen notes, 1963, and the 1,000-Yen Note Trial. He showed one-sided, enlarged and life-size copies of the 1,000-yen note. He was indicted in November 1965 under a Meiji-era counterfeiting law, found guilty in 1967 (three months in prison, suspended), lost on appeal in 1968, and the Supreme Court upheld it in 1970. The group restaged its works as evidence in court. [checked]

## Core moves
- Mimicry of civic order: copy an official campaign so exactly it turns absurd.
- Misuse of an official form: banknotes, medical exams, hotel bookings used as art material.
- Interference in daily routine: strange acts slipped into a crowded train or street.
- Excessive care: cleaning one square of pavement with lab-like seriousness.
- Trial as stage: the court case becomes part of the artwork.
- Anonymity: a collective name and a red exclamation mark instead of personal style.

## Ideas worth keeping
Hi-Red Center saw that the most effective critique of state order is often to obey it too well. A government urges citizens to make Tokyo spotless for foreign visitors; the artists respond by cleaning a tiny piece of road with absurd devotion. Nothing is broken and no slogan is shouted, yet the campaign looks ridiculous. The Shelter Plan works the same way, treating nuclear fear as a matter for polite, bureaucratic measurement.

The 1,000-yen trial pushed the question further. The court had to decide whether a copy of money was art or crime, and ruled that the risk of confusion outweighed artistic freedom. The group answered by turning the courtroom into a show, so the state itself ended up staging their work.

## What Mario could learn
- Find an official campaign and imagine following it to an excessive degree.
- Copy a bureaucratic procedure (inspection, measurement, registration) for a purpose that exposes its strangeness.
- Ask what would happen if the legal system had to decide whether your object is art.
- Make the action tiny and calm, and let the setting provide the scale.
- Do not reuse: scrubbing pavement in lab coats, measuring guests for fallout shelters, copies of yen notes, licking objects on a commuter train, the red exclamation mark.
