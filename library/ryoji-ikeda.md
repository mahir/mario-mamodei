# Ryoji Ikeda

Japanese, born 1966 in Gifu, Japan. Lives and works in Paris (some sources add Kyoto). Composer and visual artist who turns raw data, sound and light into large, precise installations.

## Who
Ikeda began as an electronic composer in the 1990s, working with sine tones and pulses at the edge of hearing. His album matrix won the Golden Nica at Ars Electronica in 2001. Since the mid-2000s he has built long-running series that treat data itself as the subject: numbers, barcodes and scientific datasets shown at a scale too large and too fast to read. His work matters because it makes the invisible material of computing into something you stand inside.

## Key works
- spectra series, from 2001. Large light installations in public spaces in cities including Amsterdam, Paris, Barcelona and Nagoya; spectra III (2008) used LED tubes and white panels. [checked]
- datamatics, from 2006. A long-running project of films, concerts and installations that try to make pure data perceptible, as flows of numbers, points and lines. [checked]
- test pattern, from 2008. A system that converts any input (text, sound, photos, video) into strobing black and white barcodes and binary patterns, testing both the limits of machines and the limits of human perception. [checked]
- the transfinite, 2011. An immersive installation in the Wade Thompson Drill Hall of the Park Avenue Armory, New York (20 May to 11 June 2011): giant screens of synchronized projected data and sound that surround the visitor. [checked]
- data-verse 1, 2019. Shown in the Arsenale at the 58th Venice Biennale. Built from open scientific data from CERN, NASA and the Human Genome Project, moving from microscopic to cosmic scale. First of a trilogy. [checked]

## Core moves
- Data as landscape: datasets shown so large they become terrain, not information.
- Overload past reading: speed and density stop you decoding and force you to feel.
- Universal conversion: one rule (to barcode, to 0 and 1) applied to every kind of input, so all content becomes the same substance.
- The threshold test: work set at the edge of what eyes, ears and screens can resolve.
- Scale sweep: zooming from atom to universe in one continuous flow.
- Strict reduction: black, white, sine tone, pulse. No decoration.

## Ideas worth keeping
Ikeda's subject is the gap between data and meaning. Our world now runs on numbers that no person ever looks at. He shows those numbers directly, but at a rate and size that make them unreadable, so the viewer meets data as a physical force, like weather. The barcode rule of test pattern is a quiet joke about how every song, face or sentence is reduced to the same stripes inside a machine.

His mathematics is also a source of wonder, not only coldness. The title the transfinite points to Cantor's idea of infinities larger than other infinities. The work asks what a human body feels next to quantities it cannot count. The Park Avenue Armory described the scale of data in that piece as one that "defies comprehension" (Park Avenue Armory program page).

## What Mario could learn
- Pick one conversion rule and apply it to everything without exception. The sameness of the output is the message.
- Show a machine's view of a thing at the machine's speed, then ask what a person can still hold.
- Use public scientific data as raw material, but let the work be about the human limit of understanding it, not about the data's content.
- Let a single number (a dataset size, a frequency, a count) stand in for the infinite.
- Do not reuse: barcode stripes as a visual signature, black and white strobing data walls, the titles datamatics, test pattern, the transfinite, data-verse, spectra, and searchlight columns of white light pointing at the sky.
