# Yoko Ono

Japanese-born, born 1933 in Tokyo. Worked in Tokyo, New York and London. Pioneer of the instruction piece, close to Fluxus.

## Who
Ono made art that exists first as a short instruction, to be carried out by someone else or only in the mind. From the late 1950s she moved the artwork from the object to the reader's imagination and to the actions of an audience. Her book Grapefruit made the instruction itself a finished work you can carry in your pocket. Later pieces keep the same core: the viewer completes the work.

## Key works
- Painting to Be Stepped On, 1961. A canvas laid on the floor, finished only when people walk on it. It turns the most respected object in art into something to wear down with feet. [checked]
- Grapefruit, 1964. A self-published book of instructions written between 1953 and 1964, printed in Tokyo on 4 July 1964 in an edition of 500 under her own imprint, Wunternaum Press. Each page is a short task, often impossible or poetic, sorted into kinds such as music, painting and event. [checked]
- Cut Piece, 1964. First staged on 20 July 1964 at Yamaichi Concert Hall, Kyoto. Ono sits still on stage while audience members come up one by one and cut away her clothing with scissors. Restaged at Carnegie Recital Hall, New York, on 21 March 1965, filmed by Albert and David Maysles. [checked]
- Ceiling Painting (YES Painting), 1966. A white ladder leads to a framed sheet of glass on the ceiling; a magnifying glass on a chain lets the climber read a tiny printed word: YES. Shown at Indica Gallery, London, 9 to 22 November 1966. [checked]
- Wish Tree, series begun 1996. Visitors write wishes on paper tags and tie them to a living tree. The wishes are collected and sent to Imagine Peace Tower (2007) on Viðey Island, Iceland. Draws on the Japanese custom of tying prayers to trees. [checked]

## Core moves
- Instruction: the work is a short text that someone else performs or imagines.
- Impossibility: tasks that cannot be done, so the work happens only in the mind.
- Participation: the audience finishes the piece, sometimes at the artist's risk.
- Exposed passivity: the artist stays still and lets the public's choices become the content.
- Reward for effort: a small private payoff (a word, a view) that you earn by climbing, bending or waiting.
- Gathering: many strangers add small private acts to one shared object.

## Ideas worth keeping
Ono saw that a sentence can hold a whole artwork, and that the best place to build it may be the reader's head. An instruction is cheap, portable and endless: it can be done once, a thousand times, or never. That moves art from making to imagining and asks who the real author is.

Cut Piece shows the dark side of handing control to an audience. The artist does nothing; the people in the room decide how far to go, and they reveal themselves. The same structure becomes gentle in Wish Tree, where many people add private hopes to a common object. Participation is not good or bad in itself. It is a mirror. One line from Grapefruit shows the scale she worked at: "Send a smell to the moon." (Smell Piece II, 1962, quoted on MoMA's Inside/Out blog).

## What Mario could learn
- Write a task whose real site is the reader's imagination, and make the impossible part the point.
- Hand one decision to the viewer and let what they choose become the work's content.
- Use scale shifts: a tiny private reward reached by a large physical effort, or a cosmic action stated as plainly as a chore.
- Make a work that only exists when copied, walked on, worn out or added to.
- Do not reuse: scissors and an audience cutting clothing; a ladder to a hidden YES; a painting meant to be stepped on; wishes tied to a tree; the title Grapefruit; "send a smell to the moon" or similar one-line moon or cloud tasks.
