# Martin Creed

British, born 1968 in Wakefield, England. Studied at the Slade, London (1986 to 1990). Turner Prize winner 2001; a leading figure of minimal, deadpan conceptual art.

## Who
Creed makes very small decisions and gives them the full weight of an artwork. Since the late 1980s he has numbered almost everything he makes, so a ball of Blu-Tack and a gallery-sized installation sit in the same list. His Turner Prize win with an empty room whose lights turn on and off made him a public symbol of the question "is this art?". He also makes music, and he often says he does not think of himself as an artist.

## Key works
- Work No. 79, 1993. A small amount of Blu-Tack kneaded into a ball and pressed against a wall. A near-invisible gesture placed where art usually hangs. [checked]
- Work No. 88, A sheet of A4 paper crumpled into a ball, 1994 to 1995. Exactly what the title says. Copies were mailed in 1995 to over 150 galleries, museums and critics through Matthew Higgs's Imprint 93. The V&A dates its copy 1994; Manchester Art Gallery's copy is dated 1995. [checked]
- Work No. 200, Half the air in a given space, 1998. Balloons fill half the volume of a room; visitors wade through them. Later versions carry new numbers (for example Work No. 965, 2008, at the Cleveland Museum of Art). [checked]
- Work No. 227: The lights going on and off, 2000. An empty gallery whose lights switch on for five seconds and off for five seconds. Shown in his 2001 Turner Prize exhibition; versions are held by Tate and MoMA. [checked]
- Work No. 850, 2008. At Tate Britain's Duveen Galleries, a runner sprinted the 86 metre hall every 30 seconds, with an equal pause of empty space in between, for about four and a half months. [checked]

## Core moves
- The numbered list: every work, large or tiny, is an entry in one running catalogue. The system flattens hierarchy.
- Title as instruction: the title is a full, literal description, so the work can be remade from the words alone.
- Almost nothing: the smallest possible change to a space or object, so attention does the work.
- The space itself as material: light, air and the gallery's emptiness become the piece.
- Rhythm and interval: on and off, run and pause. Time divided into equal beats.
- Ratio as form: "half the air" turns a measurement into a sculpture.

## Ideas worth keeping
Creed's work asks how little is enough. By choosing a common act (crumpling paper, flicking a switch) and stating it plainly, he shows that the decision and the frame make the art, not skill or material. The numbering is quietly important. It treats making as a continuous process, like breathing, where each act is logged rather than judged.

He also undoes the gallery's habits. In the lights piece, the thing usually ignored (the room, the lighting, the time you spend) becomes the only content. The runner piece does the same with motion: the empty interval between sprints is half of the work. A Tate interview records him saying: "I don't think of myself as an artist" (Tate, Turner Prize 2001 page).

## What Mario could learn
- Keep a ledger: give every output a number and treat the list itself as a work about a practice, not a pile of single pieces.
- Write titles that are complete instructions. If the title can rebuild the work, the work becomes portable and checkable.
- Find a ratio or a rule hidden in an ordinary space (half, every thirty seconds, one per wall) and let it be the whole piece.
- Treat the pause as equal to the act. Absence on a timer is a material.
- Do not reuse: numbered "Work No." titles, crumpled A4 paper, rooms half full of balloons, lights going on and off, runners through a gallery, the phrase "the whole world + the work = the whole world", neon text slogans in Creed's style.
