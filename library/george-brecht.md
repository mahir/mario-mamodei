# George Brecht

American, 1926 to 2008. Trained research chemist who became a founding figure of Fluxus and the inventor of the "event score."

## Who
Brecht worked as a chemist (at Johnson and Johnson) while taking John Cage's experimental composition class at the New School in New York in the summers of 1958 and 1959. From 1959 he wrote "event scores": very short printed instructions, often just a title and a few words, that point attention at a small everyday happening. George Maciunas, who founded Fluxus, called them "temporal readymades": like Duchamp's found objects, but found moments. Brecht's scores became a model for Fluxus and for later instruction-based art.

## Key works
- Motor Vehicle Sundown (Event), 1960. A score for a group of parked cars whose lights, horns, doors and wipers are switched on and off by chance-drawn cards, dedicated to Cage. An early, larger-scale event. [known]
- Three Aqueous Events, summer 1961. A score sent to the pianist David Tudor. Its widely reproduced text lists three states of water: ice, water, steam. Any encounter with these counts as a performance, including making a cup of tea. [checked for title and date; wording known]
- Drip Music (Drip Event), 1959 to 1962. The score arranges a source of dripping water and an empty vessel so that the water falls into it. Fluxus performers made it theatrical, pouring from the top of a ladder; Brecht himself performed it quietly at floor level at Douglass College, Rutgers, on 6 April 1963. [checked]
- Water Yam, 1963. A box designed and produced by Maciunas for Fluxus, holding Brecht's scores printed on loose cards (copies vary, roughly seventy to one hundred cards). It is a book you shuffle rather than read in order. [checked]
- Chance-Imagery, written 1957, published 1966. An essay on chance as a way to make images, issued as a Great Bear Pamphlet by Something Else Press. [checked]

## Core moves
- Instruction: a tiny text that names a happening rather than describing a show.
- Found moment: framing something that already happens (dripping, a door opening) as a work.
- Loose format: scores on separate cards, in a box, with no fixed order.
- Openness by design: the score is left as open as it can be while keeping a shape.
- Anyone can perform: the work happens whenever a reader notices it in daily life.
- Chance: cards or drawn lots decide what happens and when.

## Ideas worth keeping
Brecht shrank the artwork down to an act of noticing. A good event score does not ask you to make anything; it changes how you see something that would have happened anyway. Once you have read "ice, water, steam," the kettle in your kitchen becomes a concert hall. The score works like a lens you take home.

His quietness matters. When others turned Drip Music into a spectacle with ladders, Brecht performed it at floor level, plainly. The point is attention, not drama. He described the aim of his scores as being "left as open as it could be and still have some shape" (quoted in the Getty's Scores Project commentary). That balance, between too much and too little instruction, is the craft.

## What Mario could learn
- Write a score that makes a common moment (a melt, a shadow, a pause) into a performance the reader cannot stop noticing.
- Test how few words can still hold a shape; cut until the work almost falls apart, then stop.
- Use the format as meaning: loose cards, a list, a label, so the reader can shuffle or skip.
- Make a work that is performed by accident, long after it is read.
- Do not reuse: water dripping into a vessel; the triad ice, water, steam; car lights and horns as an orchestra; a box of event cards called after a vegetable; the phrase "temporal readymade" as a title.
