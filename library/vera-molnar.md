# Vera Molnár

Hungarian-born French artist, 1924 to 2023. Born Vera Gács in Budapest, settled in Paris after 1948. A pioneer of computer-generated art and of rule-based geometric drawing.

## Who
Molnár spent her long life testing what happens when a simple geometric order is disturbed by a small, controlled amount of chance. From 1959 she worked with an imagined computer, the "machine imaginaire", following written rules by hand. In 1968 she got access to a real computer at a research centre in Orsay and began making plotter drawings, among the earliest computer art by anyone. She kept working into her late nineties and died in December 2023, aged 99.

## Key works
- Machine imaginaire, from 1959. Not one work but a method: she wrote simple step-by-step programs and carried them out by hand, changing shapes by fixed rules, years before she had a computer. Quatre éléments distribués au hasard (1959) belongs to this period. [checked]
- Interruptions, 1968 to 1969. Plotter drawings. A grid of equal straight lines, each given a random rotation, then random patches of lines removed, leaving holes in the pattern. The V&A holds a sheet dated 1969. [checked]
- (Des)Ordres, 1974. Plotter drawing of concentric squares whose regular pattern is randomly disturbed, so the rigid grid seems to tremble. The title plays on "disorders" and "some orders". [checked]
- Lettres à ma mère, 1981 to 1990. Plotter drawings that imitate the handwriting in the weekly letters her mother sent her, a script that changed as her mother aged; some sheets were then partly drawn over by hand. [checked]
- Hommage à Dürer, 1990. One of several homage series (others on Monet and Klee) in which she rebuilt an older artist's work through program and variation. Title and year [checked]; the common account that it is based on the magic square in Dürer's Melencolia I [verify].

## Core moves
- The small disorder: about 1% randomness added to a strict system, enough to make it alive but not chaotic.
- Imagined machine: running a program by hand before owning a computer; the algorithm comes first, the hardware second.
- Variation by tiny steps: changing one parameter at a time and keeping every result, so the series is the work.
- Interruption: removing parts of a pattern so absence becomes a shape.
- Homage by algorithm: turning a predecessor's form (Dürer, Monet, Klee) into a rule and rerunning it.
- Machine with a hand: plotter output finished or disturbed by hand marks.

## Ideas worth keeping
Molnár treated the computer as a patient assistant that could try every small variation she could not try by hand. Her question was where order turns into disorder, and how little change is needed for the eye to feel it. The answer she kept returning to was very little: about one percent. That idea is a strong lesson for any rule-based work. Pure order is dead, pure chance is noise, and the interesting zone is a thin band between.

Lettres à ma mère shows that her systems were not cold. A program that imitates a mother's ageing handwriting is a portrait, a record of decline, and a love letter, all made through repetition. The Centre Pompidou notes her rule of drawing every day, "pas une journée sans une ligne" (not a day without a line).

## What Mario could learn
- Set a strict rule, then let a small, stated percentage of it fail. Name the percentage in the work.
- Write the program in words and "run" it as a human first. The written algorithm can be the artwork.
- Make a series where only one variable changes and show all the results side by side.
- Use an algorithm to hold a memory of a person (their handwriting, their habits) rather than an abstract pattern.
- Do not reuse: rotated-line grids with missing patches (Interruptions), trembling concentric squares, the phrase "1% de désordre" as a title, plotter imitations of a mother's letters, the title Machine imaginaire.
