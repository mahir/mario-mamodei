# John Cage

American, 1912 to 1992. Composer, writer and teacher in New York and at Black Mountain College. Precursor of Fluxus and Conceptual art.

## Who
Cage was a composer who changed how artists in every field thought about intention. He used chance to remove his own taste from decisions, and he treated silence as a frame for whatever sounds happened to occur. His classes at the New School in the late 1950s trained the people who made Fluxus event scores. Through them, the idea that a short written instruction can be a complete work passed into Conceptual art.

## Key works
- Music of Changes, 1951. A piano piece. Cage used chance procedures drawn from the I Ching to build charts for tempo, loudness, sounds and silences, and durations. [checked]
- Imaginary Landscape No. 4 (March No. 2), 1951. A piece for twelve radios, 24 players and a conductor; what sounds depends on what is on the air. [known]
- Theater Piece No. 1, August 1952, Black Mountain College. An unscripted event in the dining hall, often called the first Happening. The audience sat in four triangular sections while Cage spoke from a ladder; Rauschenberg, Merce Cunningham and David Tudor took part. [checked]
- 4'33", 1952. First performed on 29 August 1952 at the Maverick Concert Hall in Woodstock, New York, by David Tudor, who opened and closed the keyboard lid to mark three movements. The music is whatever the room and audience produce. [checked]
- 4'33" (In Proportional Notation), 1952 to 1953. The earliest surviving score: vertical lines where distance on the page stands for time. Cage gave it to his friend Irwin Kremen in 1953. [checked]
- Classes in experimental composition, New School for Social Research, late 1950s. George Brecht attended in the summers of 1958 and 1959; Dick Higgins, Jackson Mac Low, Allan Kaprow and Al Hansen were among the young artists shaped by the class. Brecht's event scores became a central Fluxus form. [checked]

## Core moves
- Chance: a procedure makes the choices so the author's taste steps aside.
- The frame: set a time span or a stage and let the world fill it.
- Score as text: a work can be a set of written conditions that anyone can perform.
- Attention as material: the work is a change in how you listen, not a new sound.
- Many voices at once: overlapping, unrelated actions with no single centre.
- Found broadcast: using whatever the airwaves carry at that moment.

## Ideas worth keeping
Cage's central claim is that there is no true silence. When you stop producing sound, you hear what was always there. 4'33" is less a piece of nothing than a device that turns the audience's attention back on the room. He later recalled the premiere: "You could hear the wind stirring outside during the first movement" (MoMA). The work also changes with every performance, so the score fixes a situation, not a result.

Chance mattered to him as a discipline, not a game. Letting a procedure decide is a way to meet things you would never have chosen. For artists who came after him, this opened a path: write the rule, give up control of the outcome, and treat the record of what happened as the work's evidence.

## What Mario could learn
- Design a frame (a duration, a place, a list of conditions) and let the world supply the content.
- Use a fixed procedure to make choices you cannot predict, then report honestly what it produced.
- Write works that a stranger could carry out from the text alone.
- Point attention at something already present rather than adding a new thing.
- Do not reuse: a silent timed performance in three movements, or any "4'33"" variant; a pianist opening and closing a lid; radios tuned at random as an orchestra; coin tossing with the I Ching; a lecture from a ladder; the line "I have nothing to say and I am saying it".
