# Trevor Paglen

American, born 1974. Artist and geographer who photographs secret places and studies how machines see. Works between photography, research and engineering.

## Who
Paglen makes the hidden infrastructure of power visible, or shows exactly how invisible it is: spy satellites, black sites, undersea cables, AI training datasets. His method is research first, using public data, amateur networks and long-range optics, then images that look calm and beautiful while being about surveillance. Since the mid 2010s he has focused on computer vision and AI, often working with researcher Kate Crawford. He matters because he treats seeing itself as a political act and asks what it means to see something you are not meant to.

## Key works
- The Other Night Sky, project reported in 2008 (ongoing for years). Using orbital data compiled by amateur satellite watchers such as Ted Molczan, he tracked and photographed classified US satellites, about 189 objects across some 1,500 images, so they appear as faint streaks among the stars. [checked]
- Limit Telephotography, mid 2000s (works dated 2006 and 2007). Photographs of secret military and intelligence sites taken from many miles away with astronomical telescopes, through so much air that the images shimmer and blur. One image reportedly took six years of return trips. [checked]
- Adversarially Evolved Hallucinations, 2017. Shown in A Study of Invisible Images at Metro Pictures, New York (8 September to 21 October 2017). He trained one AI to recognise images linked to themes such as omens and portents, monsters and dreams, and had a second AI generate pictures to satisfy it, producing images no human made or saw first. [checked]
- Orbital Reflector, 2018. A 100-foot, diamond-shaped reflective balloon meant to inflate in orbit as a purely aesthetic satellite, visible to the naked eye. It launched in December 2018 on a SpaceX Falcon 9 with the Nevada Museum of Art, but the US government shutdown delayed approval to deploy it, contact was lost, and the work is officially unrealised. [checked]
- ImageNet Roulette, 2019, with Kate Crawford. A website where people uploaded selfies and got labels from ImageNet's "person" categories, often racist, absurd or cruel. Linked to their exhibition Training Humans at the Osservatorio Fondazione Prada, Milan. ImageNet then announced it would remove some 600,000 images of people, and the site was taken down after 27 September 2019. [checked]

## Core moves
- Seeing the unseen: pointing a camera at what officially does not exist.
- Distance as content: the blur of miles of air becomes the subject.
- Amateur infrastructure: hobbyist data used to track state secrets.
- Training as authorship: choose the categories, let the machine make the picture.
- Classification exposed: let people feel how a dataset labels them.
- Useless object in a useful place: a satellite that does nothing but shine.

## Ideas worth keeping
Paglen shows that a picture can be true and still not reveal anything. A long-lens photo of a black site proves the site is there while making it feel more secret. A satellite streak is evidence and abstraction at once. In the AI works he flips the question from "what do we see?" to "what do machines see, and what categories did humans give them?" The training data, with its labels and taxonomies, is a worldview. ImageNet Roulette made that worldview personal by naming you.

Orbital Reflector adds a lesson about failure and power. The work did not happen because no one at the government was available to approve it. Paglen said there was "literally nobody we could call" (The Art Newspaper, 2019). The failure became the clearest picture of who controls the sky.

## What Mario could learn
- Choose a hidden category system (a dataset, a form, a code list) and let its labels become the text.
- Invent a taxonomy of feelings or omens and imagine what a machine would make of it.
- Make an image whose subject is the limit of seeing: distance, noise, blur, compression.
- Accept that a planned work may fail, and treat the record of failure as the piece.
- Do not reuse: classified satellites as star trails, telescope photos of black sites, a reflective balloon in orbit, a selfie classifier from ImageNet, a portrait of a fellow artist read by face recognition.
