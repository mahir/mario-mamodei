# Marcel Duchamp

French-born American, 1887 to 1968. Worked in Paris and New York. The main precursor of Conceptual art.

## Who
Duchamp moved the act of art from making to choosing. His readymades, his notes and his boxes showed that selecting, naming, signing and placing a thing can be the whole work. Nearly every Conceptual artist of the 1960s, from Kosuth to Baldessari, argued with him or built on him. He also treated loss, replicas and accidents as part of a work's life, not damage to it.

## Key works
- Bicycle Wheel, 1913 (original lost; MoMA holds the third version, New York, 1951). A bicycle wheel fixed upright on a kitchen stool. The 1951 replica was made for Sidney Janis's gallery: the wheel came from Europe and the stool was bought in Brooklyn. [checked]
- With Hidden Noise, 1916. A ball of twine pressed between two brass plates joined by four long screws. His patron Walter Arensberg put an unknown object inside, so the work rattles with a secret. [checked]
- Fountain, 1917 (original lost; Tate holds a 1964 replica). A urinal signed "R. Mutt 1917", sent to the Society of Independent Artists in New York, a body Duchamp helped found, and kept out of its show. The original is known only from a photograph by Alfred Stieglitz. [checked]
- 50 cc of Paris Air, 1919. An ampoule bought from a Paris pharmacist as a souvenir for Arensberg. It broke in 1949 and was repaired, which leaves open whether the air inside is still from Paris. [checked]
- The Bride Stripped Bare by Her Bachelors, Even (The Large Glass), 1915 to 1923. Eight years of work on glass. It cracked in transit in the late 1920s or early 1930s; Duchamp repaired it in 1936 and accepted the cracks. [checked]
- The Bride Stripped Bare by Her Bachelors, Even (The Green Box), 1934. A green flocked box of facsimile notes, sketches and studies from 1912 to 1917 made while planning the Large Glass, in no fixed order. Edition of 300 plus 20 deluxe copies. The Met counts 93 note templates; the Philadelphia Museum says 94 items. [checked]

## Core moves
- Selection: choosing an existing thing and calling it art.
- Signature as act: a false name turns an object into an authored work.
- The sealed secret: content no one, not even the artist, can check.
- Gift or souvenir: a trivial purchase made precious by who it is for.
- Notes as the work: the plan becomes as important as the object.
- Accepted accident: damage taken into the work instead of fixed away.
- The lost original: the work lives on through replicas and photographs.
- The rejected submission: testing an institution by sending it something it must refuse or accept.

## Ideas worth keeping
Duchamp showed that art can be a decision rather than a skill. Once a chosen object can be art, the question shifts to who decides, under what rules, and with what paperwork. Fountain is famous for a show it never appeared in; its life is a photograph, a story and later copies. That makes the record, not the thing, the carrier of the work.

He also liked works that cannot be fully known. A box of loose notes asks the reader to build the meaning. A rattle with an unknown cause asks you to accept not knowing. The ampoule of air asks whether a label is enough to keep something real. He called the cracks in the Large Glass "an improvement" (Philadelphia Museum of Art).

## What Mario could learn
- Let a single choice carry the work, then put the effort into the reasons and the record of that choice.
- Build in something unverifiable: a content, an origin or a promise the viewer must take on trust.
- Treat repair, loss and copying as events in a work's biography that can change its meaning.
- Use an institution's own rules as material: what happens when something is submitted, refused, or filed?
- Do not reuse: a signed urinal or any plumbing signed with a false name; "R. Mutt"; bottled or sealed city air; a ball of twine with a hidden object; a bicycle wheel on a stool; a box of facsimile notes; a cracked glass declared better; "The Bride Stripped Bare"; a moustache drawn on a famous portrait.
