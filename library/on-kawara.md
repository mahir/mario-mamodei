# On Kawara

Japanese, born December 1932 in Kariya, Japan (official date 2 January 1933), died 2014 in New York. Lived in New York for about fifty years; a central figure of conceptual art built on time and daily record.

## Who
Kawara spent nearly five decades turning the passing of days into art through strict, repeated systems. He painted dates, mailed the time he got up, traced where he walked, listed whom he met, and sent telegrams saying he was alive. He gave almost no interviews and avoided being photographed, so the works speak for him. His practice is the clearest example of a life lived under a self-made rule and recorded without comment.

## Key works
- Today series (Date Paintings), from 4 January 1966. Plain red, blue or gray canvases with that day's date painted in white, in the language and format of the country where he was. Each had to be finished by midnight or was destroyed. Each was stored in a box, often lined with a cutting from that day's local newspaper. Several thousand paintings over 48 years in more than 130 places. [checked]
- I Got Up, 1968 to 1979. Picture postcards mailed to friends, each rubber-stamped with the time he got out of bed that day. [checked]
- I Went, 1968 to 1979. Photocopied city maps on which he traced his route for each day in red pen. [checked]
- I Met, 1968 to 1979. Typed lists of the names of everyone he spoke with each day. [checked]
- I Am Still Alive, 1970 to 2000. Telegrams to friends and colleagues carrying the same message, "I am still alive," with a few earlier telegrams from 1969. [checked]
- One Million Years, 1970 to 1998. Two sets of volumes, Past and Future, listing a million years each, typed out year by year (Past begins in 998,031 BC; Future ends in 1,001,997 AD). Read aloud live from 1993, each reading picking up where the last stopped; a recording began at David Zwirner in 2009. Past is dedicated to all those who have lived and died, Future to the last one. ## Core moves
- The rule and its record: a fixed procedure carried out the same way, again and again.
- Duration: the work's true length is the artist's life.
- Daily proof: each piece is evidence that a day happened.
- Self-erasure: the artist appears only as times, routes and names, never as feeling.
- Deadline as form: midnight decides whether the work exists.
- Scale shift: from one day to a million years, both told in the same plain format.
- Circulation: postcards and telegrams send the record out into other people's lives.

## Ideas worth keeping
Kawara takes the most universal fact, that time passes and we are in it, and makes it concrete. A date painting says only "today," but its box of newspaper shows what else happened that day. The private and the public sit side by side, with no comment linking them.

His systems make absence loud. "I am still alive" is only true at the moment of sending; by the time it arrives it is already a claim about the past, and since his death every telegram reads differently. One Million Years makes a single human life look tiny, and yet reading the years aloud, one by one, is a human act. The work holds both the cosmic and the daily in the same flat language. Little is said, so the reader brings the meaning.

## What Mario could learn
- Invent a strict daily rule and imagine its record after decades; let the gaps and broken days speak.
- Pair one tiny personal fact with one public fact from the same moment, and leave the link unstated.
- Make a message whose truth expires in transit, so the reader receives something already outdated.
- Use counting at a scale too large for a life to reach, and give it a human voice.
- Do not reuse: a painted date on a monochrome canvas; postcards stamped with a wake-up time; maps with a daily route in red; lists of people met; telegrams saying "I am still alive"; a list of a million years; dedications to the dead or to the last one.
