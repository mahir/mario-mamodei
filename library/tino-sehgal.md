# Tino Sehgal

British-born artist, born 1976 in London, raised in France and Germany, based in Berlin. Trained in political economy and dance; makes "constructed situations" made only of live human encounter.

## Who
Sehgal makes art that leaves no object behind. His works are carried out by people he calls interpreters: dancers, guards, children, retirees, who act out a set of rules in a museum while it is open. He forbids photographs, film, wall labels and printed catalogues, and he sells the works without any paperwork at all. He won the Golden Lion at the 55th Venice Biennale in 2013. He matters because he pushed the old Conceptual idea of the "dematerialized" artwork to its legal and economic end: a work that exists only while it happens, and afterwards only in memory and talk.

## Key works
- Kiss, 2003. Two dancers in street clothes slowly move through a chain of embraces taken from famous kisses in art history (Rodin, Brancusi, Koons and others). It runs for the whole of the museum's opening hours, and MoMA owns an edition. [checked]
- This is so contemporary, 2005. Shown in the German Pavilion at the Venice Biennale, where museum guards broke into dance around visitors. [checked]
- This Progress, 2006 (first shown at the ICA, London; restaged at the Guggenheim, New York, 29 January to 10 March 2010). Visitors walking up the Guggenheim ramp are met by a child, then a teenager, then adults, each of whom asks what "progress" means and passes the talk on. The visitor learns the title only at the top. [checked]
- This is propaganda, 2002. A live work shown at the Tate Triennial; Tate acquired it in 2005, bought, like all his works, through a spoken contract. [checked]
- These Associations, 2012. The Unilever commission in Tate Modern's Turbine Hall (24 July to 28 October 2012). A large group of participants ran, walked, sang and stopped visitors to tell personal stories, sometimes blending into the crowd, sometimes forming a clear group. [checked]

## Core moves
- Withdrawal of the object: no material leftover, not even a photograph.
- The oral contract: the sale itself is spoken, before a notary and witnesses, with three to six conditions and no paper.
- Interpreters as medium: ordinary people, of chosen ages, carry the work.
- Conversation as material: a question put to a stranger is the artwork.
- Duration: the piece runs for the full opening hours, every day, like a painting on a wall.
- Hidden title: the visitor discovers the name of what they just took part in only at the end.

## Ideas worth keeping
Sehgal asks whether an economy can produce value without producing things. His answer is to make transformations of action, not of matter. The ban on documentation is not shyness. It makes the work depend on human memory and retelling, which is how songs and rituals have always lasted. The sale with no receipt makes the market itself perform the idea: the buyer pays for something that exists only as an agreement between people. As he said of his sales, "Instead of a paper certificate, there's an oral sale contract" (The Art Newspaper, 2006).

His works also turn the viewer into a participant without asking first. That raises a question about consent and attention: what happens when a museum starts to look back at you, or talk to you?

## What Mario could learn
- Ask what the work would be if no image of it were allowed. A text that can only be retold, never shown, is a strong constraint.
- Use a passed-along question: a thought that changes as it moves from one voice to another, young to old.
- Make the economic frame part of the piece: how is it owned, sold, inherited, remembered?
- Delay the naming: let the title arrive last and change what came before.
- Do not reuse: a slow-motion kiss quoting art history, guards who sing the title, a ramp of interpreters asking about "progress", the spoken sale before a notary, the "This is ..." title formula.
