# Mieko Shiomi

Japanese, born 1938 in Okayama (as Chieko Shiomi). Composer and Fluxus artist based in Osaka; spent 1964 to 1965 with Fluxus in New York.

## Who
Shiomi studied musicology in Tokyo and around 1959 to 1960 co-founded Group Ongaku, an improvising ensemble that treated everyday sounds and actions as music. In 1964 she joined Fluxus in New York, and from 1965 she ran the Spatial Poems, nine works by mail in which people around the world performed a small action at the same time and reported back. She turned the event score into a planetary network long before the internet. She later worked mainly as a composer from Osaka.

## Key works
- Event for the Late Afternoon, 1963. An outdoor event, performed in Okayama in 1964 and documented in MoMA's collection. It is usually described as a violin hung on a rope from a height and slowly lowered. [checked for title, date and place; description of the action verify]
- Mirror Piece, 1963. An event score that exists in many forms: handwritten English and Japanese versions at MoMA, a card in the Fluxus box Events and Games (c. 1964), and many later performances on beaches and in galleries. [checked]
- Water Music, 1964. A Fluxus object with two instructions on its label: give the water a still form, then let it lose that form. [checked]
- Endless Box, 1963 to 1964. A multiple of 34 paper boxes nested inside each other, folding down toward nothing. [checked]
- Spatial Poem No. 1, 1965. Shiomi mailed a "Word Event" prompt across the Fluxus network: write a word on a card and place it somewhere. Each reply became a flag on a pin stuck into a hand-drawn world map, a poem spread across the globe. [checked]
- Spatial Poems series, 1965 to 1975. Nine mail events including Direction Event (No. 2, 1966), Falling Event (No. 3, a calendar of falling actions from 24 June to 16 October 1966, with pages that can be unbolted so they fall), Shadow Event (No. 4), Open Event, Sound Event, Wind Event and Disappearing Event (No. 9). Collected in a 1975 booklet dedicated to "the people of the 30th century." A tenth version, moving event, was shown at the 2022 Aichi Triennale. [checked]

## Core moves
- Circulation: an instruction travels by post; the replies are the work.
- Simultaneity at a distance: strangers act at the same time in different places.
- Mapping: responses plotted on a world map or calendar turn private acts into a picture of a network.
- Action poem: a poem that is done, not read.
- Gradual disappearance: forms that shrink, fall, melt or fade as their content.
- Score as object: instructions printed on boxes, cards and calendars that are themselves part of the poem.

## Ideas worth keeping
Shiomi asks where a poem can be. In the Spatial Poems the poem is spread across the planet, written in the actions of people who never meet. The map is not a picture of the work; it is the poem's only visible body. This makes the gap between where the instruction was sent and where it was done into the material.

Her other pieces are about change of state over time. Water given a form and then allowed to lose it, boxes that nest down to nothing, a face or a sound that fades: she makes the vanishing the event. The 1975 dedication "to the people of the 30th century" (MoMA's post) shows her sense of scale. A small action today is addressed to readers a thousand years away.

## What Mario could learn
- Send one simple prompt to many imagined people and let the gathered replies, mapped or listed, be the piece.
- Make an artwork whose body is a distance: two places, two times, the same small act.
- Choose a change of state (still to moving, full to empty, present to gone) and make that change the whole event.
- Address a work to a reader far in the future and let that address shape what is said.
- Do not reuse: a world map of pinned word flags; a calendar of falling events; nested endless boxes; water given and then losing form; a violin lowered on a rope; the dedication to the 30th century.
