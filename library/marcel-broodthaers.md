# Marcel Broodthaers

Belgian, 1924 to 1976. Born in Brussels, died in Cologne on his 52nd birthday; also lived in Düsseldorf, Berlin and London. Poet turned artist, author of a fictional museum and a founder of institutional critique.

## Who
Broodthaers spent twenty years as a poor poet, bookseller and journalist before turning to art at forty, in 1964. His first art object was literally his last book of poems, sealed in plaster. He then spent his short career (twelve years) playing with the gap between words, images and objects, in the spirit of his countryman Magritte, and with the authority of museums. His invented Museum of Modern Art, Eagles Department, showed how a museum makes meaning by labeling, classifying and selling. He has had large influence on artists who work with display, archives and fiction.

## Key works
- Pense-Bête, 1964. He took the unsold copies of his poetry book Pense-Bête (a word for a reminder note, with "bête" meaning beast or stupid) and set them in plaster on a base, so the poems became a sculpture that could be seen but not read. [known]
- Exhibition announcement, 1964. For his first show at Galerie Saint-Laurent in Brussels he wrote that he, too, wondered if he could sell something and succeed in life, a frank text that framed art as a trade. [known; quote checked via a secondary source]
- Musée d'Art Moderne, Département des Aigles, 1968 to 1972. A fictional museum with no collection and no fixed home, opened in his Brussels house in 1968. It existed as a series of "sections" in different places: postcards of paintings, empty art shipping crates, wall texts, film. [checked]
- Section Financière, 1970 to 1971. He offered the whole museum for sale "on account of bankruptcy" on the cover of the 1971 Cologne Art Fair catalogue; no buyer came. He also issued gold bars stamped with an eagle, priced at twice the market value of gold, the extra being the price of "art." [checked]
- Section des Figures (Der Adler vom Oligozän bis heute), Düsseldorf, 1972. Hundreds of eagle images and objects borrowed from museums, from ancient to modern, each labeled "This is not a work of art." The catalogue came in two volumes, with a coupon to redeem the second. Shown before the museum closed at documenta 5, Kassel, 1972. [checked for Düsseldorf 1972 and documenta 5 and the label; title spelling verify]
- Open letters, from 1968. Printed letters on Département des Aigles letterhead, sent to friends and the art world, in which the museum's "director" commented on politics, art and his own fiction. [known]

## Core moves
- Fiction as institution: invent an official body and give it letterhead, sections and a director.
- Misuse of an official form: catalogues, labels, letters and sale notices as the art.
- Language as object: a text made unreadable becomes a thing.
- The label that denies: "This is not a work of art" placed on everything.
- Inventory: the same motif (the eagle) collected across all of history.
- Price as content: gold bars marked up to show what "art value" costs.
- Self-irony: the artist openly admits he wants to sell.

## Ideas worth keeping
Broodthaers saw that a museum is not just a building but a grammar: labels, numbers, crates, catalogues, a director's voice. If you copy that grammar you can make a museum out of almost nothing, and in doing so show how much of art's value comes from the frame. His eagle, the oldest symbol of power, becomes a joke about authority. Every eagle gets the same label, so the label, not the object, is what we are looking at.

His start as an artist is a lesson in turning failure into material. The unsold poems became his first sculpture. The honest admission that he hoped to sell something made the market part of the subject. He mixed sincerity and irony so closely that you cannot pull them apart, which is why his work still unsettles.

## What Mario could learn
- Invent an institution (a department, an archive, a bureau) and let its forms and letterhead carry the whole work.
- Take a failed or unsold thing and make its failure the material of a new work.
- Collect one motif across history and put the same blunt label on every example.
- Make price, sale or bankruptcy part of what the work says.
- Do not reuse: a fictional museum of eagles; "This is not a work of art" labels; books sealed in plaster; gold bars marked up for art value; selling a museum due to bankruptcy; open letters from a fictional museum director.
