# Tehching Hsieh

Taiwanese-American, born 1950 in Taiwan. Arrived in New York in the 1970s after jumping ship at age 24, and made his major works there while living without legal papers. Known for year-long performances.

## Who
Hsieh made five performances that each lasted exactly one year, followed by a thirteen-year project, between 1978 and 1999. Each one is a single rule applied to his whole life for a fixed period, documented with deadpan rigor (signed statements, witnesses, punch cards, photos, maps). He worked as an undocumented immigrant with almost no English and no art career, which gives his rules about confinement, time and the city a weight beyond art. He matters because he made the duration of a life itself the material.

## Key works
- One Year Performance 1978 to 1979 (Cage Piece). He lived alone in a cage built in his New York studio for a year, without speaking, reading, or contact with the outside world. A friend brought food and took away waste. A lawyer sealed the cage and certified the year. [checked]
- One Year Performance 1980 to 1981 (Time Clock Piece). He punched a worker's time clock every hour on the hour for a year and took one film frame each time, about 8,760 frames that make a six-minute film of his hair growing. [checked]
- One Year Performance 1981 to 1982 (Outdoor Piece). He stayed outdoors in New York for a year and never entered a building, subway, car or other shelter. He kept daily maps of his movements; the surviving record includes two arrest records. [checked]
- Art/Life One Year Performance 1983 to 1984 (Rope Piece), with Linda Montano. The two were tied together at the waist by an eight-foot rope for a year and agreed not to touch. [checked]
- One Year Performance 1985 to 1986 (No Art Piece). For a year he did not make, look at, read about or talk about art. [checked]
- Thirteen Year Plan, 1986 to 1999. He made art but did not show it publicly for thirteen years. It ended at the turn of the millennium with a report that he had kept himself alive. [checked]

## Core moves
- Duration: the length of time is the work, and a year is a measured unit of life.
- The rule and its record: a simple self-imposed law plus dry evidence that it was kept.
- Endurance: not drama but steady repetition.
- Withdrawal: removing art, speech or shelter to see what life is without it.
- Bureaucratic proof: lawyers, signatures, clocks, as if art had to pass an audit.
- Invisibility: the last work hides itself for thirteen years.

## Ideas worth keeping
Hsieh shows that a life can be framed as a piece just by setting a rule and a start and end date. The rules are almost nothing (stay in, punch in, stay out, stay tied, stay away), yet each one isolates a basic condition: prison, wage labor, homelessness, dependence on another person, the separation of art and life. Because he was undocumented, these are not abstract. The time clock is a picture of the worker's day; the street is the place where an illegal migrant actually lives.

His work also questions what counts as evidence. We cannot watch a year. We only see the receipts. The art sits in the gap between the claim and the record, and in the trust the record asks for. He described the last project as a time of "surviving," then stated he "kept myself alive" (Neue Nationalgalerie wall texts, 2023).

## What Mario could learn
- Choose one rule and one fixed period, then ask what the rule reveals about a social condition.
- Make the documentation as plain and official as possible, and let the plainness do the emotional work.
- Think about subtraction: what single activity, if removed for a long time, would change a life most?
- Consider how an image can stand for a stretch of time it cannot show.
- Do not reuse: living in a cage for a year, punching a time clock every hour, a year outdoors, two people tied by a rope, a year without art, a thirteen-year hidden practice, the phrase "I kept myself alive."
