# JODI

Artist duo formed in 1994: Joan Heemskerk (Dutch, born 1968) and Dirk Paesmans (Belgian, born 1965 in Brussels). Based in the Netherlands. Pioneers of net art in the mid-1990s.

## Who
Heemskerk and Paesmans trained in photography and video, then spent a residency at San Jose State University from 1993, close to Silicon Valley, where they met the early web. Their site jodi.org, set up in 1995, looked broken on purpose: flashing text, scrambled characters, pages that seemed to crash the browser. They treat software, games and the computer desktop as raw material, exposing the machinery that interfaces try to hide. MoMA calls them among the first artists to make art for the internet.

## Key works
- wwwwwwwww.jodi.org, 1995. Their main website, which Rhizome treats as one large work containing many smaller ones. A sprawling set of pages built from basic HTML that read like a system gone wrong. Site and year [checked] (Rhizome); the visual description is general.
- Automatic Rain, 1995. An early browser piece over three pages, inspired by timed sprinklers on Silicon Valley lawns, using Netscape's blink tag and slow loading. It no longer works properly in modern browsers. [checked]
- Untitled Game, 1996 to 2001. A set of modifications of the shooter game Quake. One version, "Arena", is entirely white, so the game's space and sounds remain but nothing can be seen. [checked]
- SOD, 1999. A modification of Wolfenstein 3D that strips the game down to a black, white and grey maze of abstract shapes. [checked]
- My%Desktop, 2002. Four-channel video of the artists working a Mac OS 9 desktop so fast and so wrongly that windows, files and error alerts pile up like a virus. In MoMA's collection. [checked]
- A widely repeated story says the source code of the jodi.org home page hides a diagram of a bomb. [verify] Not confirmed in any source read for this note.

## Core moves
- Error as image: crashes, garbage characters and broken layout used as the visible artwork.
- Show the source: making the code, not the picture, the real surface.
- Mod and strip: taking a commercial game and removing everything except its bare structure.
- Misuse of the interface: operating normal tools in abnormal ways until they reveal their logic.
- Built-in obsolescence: work made for a specific browser or system that decays as the tech changes.
- The screen as stage: recording desktop actions as performance.

## Ideas worth keeping
JODI's core insight is that every smooth interface is a costume over a messy machine. By breaking the costume, they show the machine's grammar: tags, loops, alerts, coordinates. Their work feels hostile or confusing at first, which is the point. It reminds users that they do not control the computer as fully as they think, and that the computer does not "understand" them either.

Their pieces also age in public. Automatic Rain depended on a blink tag that later browsers dropped, so the work now exists partly as history and documentation. This makes software art a kind of weather: dependent on conditions that change. Josephine Bosma remembered its "bright blue cypher columns" that "moved, blinked, and trickled down" (Rhizome Net Art Anthology).

## What Mario could learn
- Let the machine's own failure be the voice. An error message, read as poetry, can be a statement about the limits of communication.
- Expose the layer under the surface: the code, the log, the metadata, the alt text.
- Take a familiar system and subtract until only its rules are left.
- Accept that a digital work will rot, and design that decay into its meaning.
- Do not reuse: chaotic green-on-black HTML pages, all-white game levels, stripped Wolfenstein or Quake mods, desktop screen-recordings of piling error windows, the titles My%Desktop, SOD, Untitled Game, Automatic Rain, and the "bomb diagram hidden in the source" set-up.
