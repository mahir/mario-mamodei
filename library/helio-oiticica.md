# Hélio Oiticica

Brazilian, 1937 to 1980. Rio de Janeiro artist who moved from Neo-Concrete painting to wearable capes, walk-in environments and immersive slide works. Showed in London in 1969 and lived in New York for much of the 1970s before returning to Rio.

## Who
Oiticica took color off the wall and put it on the body and into the street. Through friends at the Mangueira samba school in a Rio favela he found a public and a way of moving that changed his art. His installation Tropicália gave its name to the Tropicália movement in Brazilian music. Under the dictatorship his work also took the side of people the state treated as criminals.

## Key works
- Metaesquemas, 1950s (for example Metaesquema 362, 1958). Small gouaches of shifting colored rectangles, the starting point he later left behind. [checked]
- B33 Box Bólide 18, Homage to Cara de Cavalo, 1965 to 1966. A box holding a newspaper photo of his friend Cara de Cavalo, a Mangueira man killed by police. [checked]
- Parangolés, from 1965. Capes and banners of layered fabric and plastic made to be worn while dancing samba. The color only comes alive when a body moves in it. P15 Parangolé Cape 11, I Embody Revolt, 1967, is one example. [checked]
- Tropicália, 1966 to 1967, first shown in Rio in 1967. Two walk-in huts, sand, plants and a parrot, ending at a television, playing on the cliché of Brazil as tropical paradise. [checked]
- Seja marginal, seja herói (Be an Outlaw, Be a Hero), 1968 (the Philadelphia Museum gives 1967, shown 1968). A red banner with the figure of the dead Cara de Cavalo. When musicians displayed it at a concert it was used against them and they were later arrested and exiled. [checked]
- Eden, 1969. At the Whitechapel Gallery, London, an environment of sand, straw, "nests" to lie in and a tent with music, built for what he called "creleisure" (creative leisure). [checked]
- Cosmococa CC5 Hendrix-War, 1973, with Neville D'Almeida. Slides and hammocks in a dark room. [checked]

## Core moves
- Color as event: color exists in time and motion, not on a flat surface.
- Wearing the work: the viewer becomes the support and the performer.
- Environment: a space you enter and use, not a thing you look at.
- Siding with the margin: the outcast as hero, the favela as center.
- Leisure as resistance: lying down, idling and dancing as a refusal of productive order.
- Cliché turned back: use a national stereotype until it shows its own falseness.

## Ideas worth keeping
Oiticica asked where a painting ends. His answer was that it ends in a body, a neighborhood and a feeling. The Parangolé makes no sense on a hanger. It needs a dancer, music and a crowd. This moves art away from contemplation toward taking part, and it puts working-class culture at the center instead of treating it as subject matter.

His banner for Cara de Cavalo shows the cost of that move. Mourning a man the state called a bandit was a political act, and the image was treated as one. A Tropicália penetrable carries the phrase "Purity is a myth" (Whitney, Hélio Oiticica: To Organize Delirium), a fair summary of his whole stance.

## What Mario could learn
- Imagine a work that only reads correctly when someone uses it, and design images that show it mid-use.
- Ask who a society calls a criminal, and what it would mean to honor them in public.
- Treat idleness and rest as a subject, not an absence.
- Take a tourist stereotype of a place and build it so literally that it turns strange.
- Do not reuse: wearable samba capes, a tropical hut with a parrot and a TV, "Be an outlaw, be a hero," nests for creative leisure, the term "creleisure," slide works with hammocks.
