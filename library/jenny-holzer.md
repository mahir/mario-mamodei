# Jenny Holzer

American, born 1950 in Gallipolis, Ohio. Works in New York State. Uses text in public places, on posters, LED signs, stone benches and light projections, to make language itself the artwork.

## Who
Holzer came to New York in the late 1970s and joined the Whitney Independent Study Program, where the long reading list of theory led her to write Truisms: hundreds of one-line statements that sound like proverbs but contradict each other. She pasted them anonymously on the streets. She then moved her words onto the media of power and commerce: electronic signs, plaques, benches, T-shirts, and from 1996 huge projections on buildings and landscapes. She represented the United States at the 1990 Venice Biennale and won the Golden Lion for best pavilion. Her subjects are power, violence, sex, war and death.

## Key works
- Truisms, 1977 to 1979. Short anonymous statements in plain capital letters, arranged alphabetically and first printed as offset posters pasted around Manhattan. Each line sounds certain; together they cancel each other out. Later on LED signs, benches, caps and T-shirts. [checked]
- Inflammatory Essays, 1979 to 1982. Twenty-line, roughly hundred-word texts in the voice of an angry manifesto, printed on bright colored paper and posted in the street. They borrow the tone of radicals from many sides, without saying whose. Tate holds an untitled work dated 1979 to 1982 and has a video on the series. [known; dates consistent with Tate listing]
- Times Square Spectacolor sign, 1982. Her first large electronic sign, showing Truisms on the giant animated board in Times Square, which made LED signs central to her work. [known; date confirmed by a secondary source]
- Survival, 1983 to 1985. Shorter, urgent lines written for signs and plaques, meant for public spaces under threat. [known]
- Laments, 1989. Stone sarcophagi and LED signs carrying the voices of thirteen imagined dead people, including children. [known; date confirmed by a secondary source]
- Light projections, from 1996. Scrolling text projected at night onto buildings, rivers and landmarks, often using other writers' poems and, later, declassified government documents. [known; start date confirmed by a secondary source]
- Redaction Paintings, from 2006 (shown widely from 2008). Large paintings that reproduce declassified US government documents about war, with the censor's black bars intact. [verify exact start year]

## Core moves
- Language as object: the sentence is the work, set in type, light or stone.
- Anonymous voice: no author named, so the reader must decide who is speaking.
- Contradiction by accumulation: many certain statements that cannot all be true.
- Hijacking the medium of power: ad signs, plaques and official forms carry personal or critical texts.
- Public placement: the work meets people who did not come to see art.
- Scale and speed: words that scroll, flash or cover a building change how they are read.
- Found official text: real redacted documents presented as paintings.

## Ideas worth keeping
Holzer saw that the most powerful voice in a city is the one with no face: the sign, the slogan, the warning. If you borrow that voice and fill it with sentences that are too honest, too extreme or too contradictory, people stop and doubt the whole system of public speech. A Truism is a test. You agree with one, then the next one says the opposite, and you realize you were agreeing with a tone, not a thought.

Her later work turns to grief and to what the state hides. A projection of a poem on a riverbank is temporary and huge; a redaction painting makes absence visible in black bars. Both ask what may be said in public, and by whom. As one Truism puts it, "Lack of charisma can be fatal." (as quoted on Wikipedia's Holzer page). The flat, confident tone is the material.

## What Mario could learn
- Write in a borrowed anonymous voice (a warning sign, a public notice, a label) and make it say something that voice never says.
- Build a set of statements that each sound true and together undo each other.
- Think about where a sentence will be met: on a wall at night, on a bench, scrolling past; let the place change the meaning.
- Use what is blacked out or missing from an official text as the image.
- Do not reuse: alphabetical lists of one-line truisms; colored-paper twenty-line manifestos; scrolling LED text in a museum spiral or Times Square; text projected on buildings and water; engraved stone benches with slogans; redacted documents as paintings; the line "Protect me from what I want."
