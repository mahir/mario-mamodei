# Lucy R. Lippard

American, born 1937 in New York City. Critic, curator, writer and activist; the chronicler of Conceptual art.

## Who
Lippard named and recorded Conceptual art as it happened. With John Chandler she described the "dematerialization" of art in 1968, and her book Six Years became the standard record of the period. Her "number shows" used card catalogues and city-wide sites to make exhibitions that behaved like the art in them. She later turned to feminist and political art, co-founding Printed Matter and the Heresies collective, and wrote widely on place and land.

## Key works
- "The Dematerialization of Art", with John Chandler, 1968. An essay in Art International, February 1968, pages 31 to 36, describing art that moves away from the object toward ideas and processes. [checked]
- Number 7, 1969. A show at Paula Cooper's gallery in New York, May 1969, with 39 artists; Lippard called herself the "compiler". [checked]
- 557,087, 1969. Seattle World's Fair Pavilion, sponsored by the Contemporary Art Council of the Seattle Art Museum. Works were spread across the city within about fifty miles, and the catalogue was a set of loose index cards to read in any order. The title was the city's approximate population. [checked]
- 955,000, 1970. The Vancouver version, at the Vancouver Art Gallery and the UBC Student Union, with more cards and three more artists. [checked]
- 2,972,453, Centro de Arte y Comunicación, Buenos Aires. Dated 1971 by Tate Papers; some accounts give late 1970 [verify]. [checked]
- c. 7,500, 1973 to 1974. Opened at CalArts, Valencia, then toured seven venues ending in London. Tate Papers says its 26 artists included many women; it is often described as an all-women show [verify]. [checked]
- Six Years: The Dematerialization of the Art Object from 1966 to 1972, 1973. A book built as an annotated chronology of works, texts and events, published by Praeger in New York (University of California Press edition 1997). [checked]

## Core moves
- Chronology as argument: a year-by-year list that makes the case by accumulation.
- The card catalogue: loose cards that readers shuffle or discard.
- Population as title: a number that names the place without naming it.
- Dispersed exhibition: works scattered across a city rather than in one room.
- Compiler, not curator: a modest role that hands authority to artists.
- Naming a tendency: a term coined at the right time shapes what follows.

## Ideas worth keeping
Lippard showed that writing about art can be shaped like the art it describes. Six Years is not a smooth story but a dense chronology, full of documents and fragments, that lets readers draw their own lines. The card catalogues for her number shows did the same thing in a show: no fixed order, no master text, the reader decides. She later admitted she had "never become a proper curator" (Tate Papers), and that refusal was a method.

Her term "dematerialization" was never quite literal (the works still used paper, photos and walls). But the word caught a real shift in where art lived: in ideas, in documents, in circulation. Her later turn to feminism and local place shows the same question asked politically: who gets recorded, and where?

## What Mario could learn
- Let the format of a record (cards, a chronology, a file) carry an argument.
- Title a work with a number or statistic that stands in for a place or a population.
- Spread a work across many sites and let the catalogue be the only place it gathers.
- Take a modest role (compiler, clerk, witness) and make that role part of the work.
- Do not reuse: exhibitions titled with a city's population; index-card catalogues read in any order; the phrase "dematerialization of the art object"; "Six Years" as a framing; a show called "c. 7,500".
