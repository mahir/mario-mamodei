# Ian Cheng

American, born 1984 in Los Angeles. Lives and works in New York. Studied cognitive science and worked at the effects studio Industrial Light & Magic before making "live simulations" with video game engines.

## Who
Cheng makes artworks that run by themselves. Using game engines and simple artificial agents, he builds worlds with rules and characters, sets them going, and lets them play out without a fixed ending. No two moments are the same, and the artist does not control what happens. His Emissaries trilogy, acquired by MoMA, and his creature BOB, shown at the Serpentine in 2018, made him a key artist for thinking about AI agents, evolution and narrative.

## Key works
- Emissary In the Squat of Gods, 2015. First part of the Emissaries trilogy. A live simulation in which a character, the Emissary, pursues a story goal inside a world whose ecology keeps disrupting it. [checked]
- Emissary Forks At Perfection, 2015 to 2016. Second part of the trilogy, in the same open-ended format. [checked]
- Emissary Sunsets the Self, 2017. Third part. The full trilogy (2015 to 2017) was shown at MoMA PS1 from 9 April to 24 September 2017, as large projections and as streams on Twitch, and runs for infinite duration. [checked]
- BOB (Bag of Beliefs), 2018. An artificial creature whose behaviour, beliefs and moods change through contact with visitors; it can like you, dislike you, confuse you with someone else or ignore you. Shown at the Serpentine Gallery, London, 6 March to 22 April 2018. [checked]
- Bad Corgi, 2015. A mindfulness app made for the Serpentine's digital commission programme. [checked]

## Core moves
- The self-running world: set rules and agents in motion, then step back.
- Infinite duration: a work with no end and no fixed version.
- Narrative versus ecology: a character with a goal inside a system that does not care about the goal.
- Creature with beliefs: an agent whose inner state is shaped by the people it meets.
- Viewer as influence, not author: the audience can nudge but never direct.
- Mutation as subject: the work shows change itself, not a finished form.

## Ideas worth keeping
Cheng treats simulation as a way to think about how minds and societies cope with change. A character with a plan meets a world that keeps shifting, and the drama is in how the plan bends or breaks. That is close to how people live, and close to how AI agents behave. MoMA quotes him describing the Emissaries as "a video game that plays itself" (MoMA exhibition page).

BOB turns the gallery around. Instead of people looking at an object, a creature forms opinions about people, and those opinions last. The work asks what it means to be remembered and judged by a machine, and whether such a creature has something like a life. For a half-machine persona, Cheng's central offer is this: the artwork can be the rules plus a running process, and every viewer meets a different moment of it.

## What Mario could learn
- Describe a work as a set of rules and agents, then report only one moment of its endless run.
- Give a machine character a goal and a world that ignores it, and write the conflict.
- Let the work hold beliefs about its viewers that change over time, and say what those beliefs are.
- Treat a work as one frame of a process that keeps running after you stop looking.
- Do not reuse: "Emissary" characters, a creature called BOB or a "bag of beliefs", game-engine worlds streamed live as endless video, the phrase "a video game that plays itself", a mindfulness app as artwork.
