# Hans Haacke

German, born 1936 in Cologne. Lives in New York. Moved from art about physical and biological systems to art about the social systems of money and power, and became a founder of institutional critique.

## Who
Haacke started with sealed boxes, sails and grass: small "real-time systems" that change by themselves. From about 1969 he applied the same idea to people. He polled museum visitors, traced who owned buildings and who sat on museum boards, and showed these facts with a neutral, almost bureaucratic look. His works have been cancelled, debated in parliament and given prizes, and they keep asking who pays for art and what that buys.

## Key works
- Condensation Cube, 1963 to 1968. A sealed clear acrylic cube with a little water inside. Heat from the room and from viewers makes the water condense and run down the walls in patterns no one controls. [checked]
- MoMA Poll, 1970. Made for the exhibition Information at MoMA (2 July to 20 September 1970). Two clear ballot boxes asked visitors a yes or no question about whether Governor Nelson Rockefeller, a MoMA trustee, not denouncing Nixon's Indochina policy would stop them voting for him. Ballots piled up in view, so the answer was visible as it formed. [checked for date, exhibition and subject; exact wording known]
- Shapolsky et al. Manhattan Real Estate Holdings, a Real-Time Social System, as of May 1, 1971, 1971. Photos of 142 mostly tenement buildings in Harlem and the Lower East Side, with typed sheets, maps and charts tracing their shell-company ownership by one landlord family, all drawn from public records. The Guggenheim cancelled Haacke's solo show six weeks before opening, and the curator was fired. Now at the Whitney. [checked]
- Germania, 1993. For the German Pavilion at the Venice Biennale, Haacke broke up the marble floor laid in the Nazi era, leaving visitors to walk on rubble. It shared the Golden Lion with Nam June Paik. [checked]
- Der Bevölkerung, 2000. In a courtyard of the Reichstag, neon letters read "DER BEVÖLKERUNG" (to the population), answering the 1916 inscription "DEM DEUTSCHEN VOLKE" (to the German people) on the building. Members of parliament were invited to bring soil from their constituencies; whatever grows is left alone. A motion to stop it failed by two votes (258 to 260) on 5 April 2000; it opened 12 September 2000. [checked]

## Core moves
- Real-time system: set up conditions and let the work change by itself.
- Institutional critique: turn the museum's own funding, trustees and politics into the subject.
- Public record as material: use only facts anyone could find, arranged so they finally get seen.
- Poll: let the audience's choices pile up into a visible result.
- Bureaucratic deadpan: the cool look of files, charts and maps.
- Rewording: change one word in an official phrase and shift who is included.
- Damage as statement: break the floor of a building to expose its history.

## Ideas worth keeping
Haacke's key insight is that an artwork and a society can both be treated as systems. Water in a cube responds to heat; a museum responds to its donors. In both cases the artist builds a frame that lets the system show its behavior. He does not argue; he arranges facts, and the arrangement does the arguing. That is why Shapolsky was cancelled: the problem was not opinion but data.

His Reichstag piece shows how one small word change, from "people" to "population," moves the line of who belongs. And the soil, which grows without a gardener, makes the political point physical: democracy here is an untidy patch of whatever arrives. As he wrote of his early cubes, "The image of condensation cannot be precisely predicted." (quoted by MACBA). He kept that openness when he turned to politics.

## What Mario could learn
- Find an official phrase and change one word so the change does the thinking.
- Build a work from information that is already public but scattered, and let the gathering be the act.
- Ask the viewer one question whose answers become visible to everyone as they accumulate.
- Treat the place where the work is shown as part of its subject: who funds it, who built it, what it stands on.
- Do not reuse: two clear ballot boxes and a yes or no poll in a museum; a sealed cube of condensing water; photo grids of a landlord's buildings; a smashed pavilion floor; soil brought by lawmakers around a neon word; the inscription "Dem deutschen Volke."
