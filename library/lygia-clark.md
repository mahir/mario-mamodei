# Lygia Clark

Brazilian, 1920 to 1988. Neo-Concrete artist in Rio de Janeiro who moved from painting to objects handled by the public, then to body-based therapy sessions. Lived in Paris from 1968 into the 1970s.

## Who
Clark started as a geometric abstract painter and ended by giving up the art object almost completely. Step by step she handed the work to the viewer: first hinged metal sculptures to fold, then a paper strip to cut, then wearable and sensory objects, and finally one-to-one sessions with clients. MoMA's 2014 retrospective was titled The Abandonment of Art, 1948 to 1988. She matters because she treated the viewer's own act and body as the true material of art.

## Key works
- Bichos (Critters), 1960 to 1963. Hinged aluminum plates that the viewer folds into new shapes. The sculpture has no fixed form and seems to answer back. [checked]
- Caminhando (Walking), 1963. A paper Möbius strip, scissors and glue. The participant cuts along the strip, choosing each time where to go, until the path is too thin to cut. Nothing remains to keep. [checked]
- A Casa é o Corpo: penetração, ovulação, germinação, expulsão (The House is the Body), 1968. A walk-through structure in which visitors pass through stages named after conception and birth, then leave "expelled." [checked]
- Sensory objects (Objetos sensoriais) and the group Nostalgia do corpo, from 1966. Masks, gloves, bags and goggles that change how the wearer feels and senses. [checked]
- Estruturação do self (Structuring the Self), 1970s and 1980s. Private therapy-like sessions using "relational objects," plain things such as bags of air, sand or water, pressed against a client's body. [checked]

## Core moves
- Participation: the work does not exist until someone acts.
- The act as the work: no object survives, only the experience of doing.
- Choice inside a rule: a simple instruction (cut along the strip) that still leaves every decision to the person.
- Body as site: the skin, breath and weight of the participant replace the canvas.
- Withdrawal of the author: the artist steps back until she is a guide, not a maker.
- Care as form: art moves toward healing and touch.

## Ideas worth keeping
Clark asked what is left of art if you remove the object, the author and the spectator. Her answer was the act itself. In Caminhando the cut has no right answer and leaves nothing behind, so the meaning sits entirely in the doing and in time. MoMA's audio guide quotes her: "The act is what produces the Caminhando" (MoMA, Lygia Clark audio guide).

Her path also shows that a radical idea can move slowly. Each stage removes one more layer between art and life. The late therapy work raises an open question: if the experience is real but private and unrecorded, is it still art, and does that matter?

## What Mario could learn
- Write an instruction whose result cannot be predicted by the person who writes it.
- Make a work whose only record is a change in the participant, then think about how an image could hint at that change without showing it.
- Use simple materials (paper, air, water) because they carry no prestige and let attention fall on the act.
- Ask what one more layer could be removed from a familiar form of art.
- Do not reuse: the cut Möbius strip, hinged metal "critters," a house-as-body walkthrough with birth stages, therapeutic sessions with bags of air.
