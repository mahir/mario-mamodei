# Tucumán Arde

Argentine collective action, 1968, in Rosario and Buenos Aires, by artists of the avant-garde group from Rosario working with journalists, sociologists and a trade union. Includes Graciela Carnevale's locked-room action of the same year.

## Who
Tucumán Arde (Tucumán Is Burning) was a joint project that set out to expose what the military government of Juan Carlos Onganía was hiding about the province of Tucumán, where sugar mills were being shut under a plan called "Operativo Tucumán." The group called the official cover-up "Operation Silence." Leading members included Graciela Carnevale, León Ferrari, Roberto Jacoby and Norberto Puzzolo, with Juan Pablo Renzi, Eduardo Favario, María Teresa Gramuglio, Nicolás Rosa and others. It is often cited as the moment when Latin American conceptual art left the art world for politics. Its archive, kept by Carnevale, is now held by MACBA in Barcelona.

## Key works
- Tucumán Arde, 1968. In three phases: research trips to Tucumán to record conditions; a staged media campaign of posters and graffiti in Rosario and Santa Fe, plus posters for a fake "First Avant-garde Art Biennial"; then an exhibition at the CGT union headquarters in Rosario in early November 1968, with a version in Buenos Aires that opened 25 November and was closed within hours. Visitors walked over the names of mill owners on the floor, lights went off every two minutes, and they were served bitter coffee without sugar. [checked]
- Graciela Carnevale, Acción del encierro (Confinement Action), 1968, Rosario, part of the group's Ciclo de Arte Experimental. She invited people to an opening in an empty gallery whose glass front was covered with posters, locked them in and left. After more than an hour a passerby broke the glass from outside to free them, and police then closed the show. One account dates it 9 October 1968. [checked]
- The banner "Visit Tucumán, garden of poverty," a parody of a tourism slogan, used in the exhibition. [checked]

## Core moves
- Counter-information: art as a rival news service against official silence.
- Overinformation: borrow the methods of advertising and the press to plant a question before the answer.
- Changing the venue: move from the gallery to a union hall, from art audience to workers.
- Sensory fact: turn statistics into a felt condition (darkness, bitter coffee, names underfoot).
- Confinement: put the audience in the position of the oppressed, so they must choose how to get out.
- Leaving art: the end point is that the artists stop calling it art.

## Ideas worth keeping
Tucumán Arde argued that if the avant-garde is serious about changing perception, it must aim at the most important perception of the day: what the state is hiding. The members used every tool they had (photos, interviews, posters, slogans, a union building) and treated the exhibition as one step in a larger action. León Ferrari's line sums it up: "Tucumán Arde used art to do politics" (MACBA record). After it, most members left art for activism.

Carnevale's locked room asks a harder question about the audience. People inside had to decide whether to break glass, wait, or ask for help, and in the end a stranger did the violent act for them. The work holds up a mirror to passivity under a dictatorship, and to who carries the risk of acting.

## What Mario could learn
- Treat an exhibition as one phase in a longer act that begins and ends outside the gallery.
- Turn a hidden fact into a bodily sensation the visitor cannot argue with.
- Design a situation where the audience must decide something, and the decision is the content.
- Use the language of advertising or tourism to say the opposite of what it normally says.
- Do not reuse: locking visitors in a gallery, bitter unsweetened coffee as a sign of a sugar crisis, names of owners on the floor, "garden of poverty," a city-wide teaser that names a burning province.
