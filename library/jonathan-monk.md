# Jonathan Monk

British, born 1969 in Leicester. Studied at Leicester Polytechnic and Glasgow School of Art; lives and works in Berlin. Remakes the classics of Conceptual and Minimal art with humour and his own family history.

## Who
Monk's main material is other artists' work, above all the Conceptual and Minimal artists of the 1960s and 1970s: Sol LeWitt, Ed Ruscha, Lawrence Weiner, Bruce Nauman, Jeff Koons. He copies, inverts, finishes or deflates their famous gestures, and often threads his own life (his parents, his holidays, his dinners) into them. He made appropriation a personal, affectionate, slightly cheeky dialogue with art history. He matters because he shows that Conceptual art's rules are themselves material that can be played with, and that a homage can also be a critique.

## Core moves
- Appropriation: borrowing a famous work and changing one thing.
- Inversion: photograph the gaps, not the buildings; deflate the inflated.
- Completing the system: carry out every permutation the rule allows.
- Family as material: the father's slides, the mother and sister as measured distance.
- Delegated damage: another artist breaks the sculpture, and that is the authorship.
- Price as text: a neon reading "Do Not Pay More Than $60,000" (2009) makes the sale conditions the work.

## Ideas worth keeping
Monk treats art history like a family album. The heroes of Conceptual art are older relatives whose habits he imitates and teases. Inverting Ruscha (empty spaces instead of buildings) asks what the original left out. Pairing LeWitt's cubes with a dance count turns a cold system into something human and rhythmic. Busts with noses broken by famous artists poke at how much value a signature adds to an act of vandalism.

He states the method plainly: "Appropriation is something I have used or worked with in my art since starting art school in 1987" (Lisson Gallery). The lesson is that copying is not empty if the copy carries a new question.

## What Mario could learn
- Take a known conceptual rule and run it on a new, personal subject, so the rule meets a life.
- Invert a classic: show what the famous work left out.
- Complete a system the original only sketched, and note what the full set reveals.
- Put the price, contract or condition of the work into the work.
- Do not reuse: the gaps between Sunset Strip buildings, deflating Koons rabbits, a father's slides of Gregory Peck, busts with noses broken by named artists, the "Meeting #" format, the neon price cap.
