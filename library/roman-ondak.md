# Roman Ondák

Slovak, born 1966. Lives and works in Bratislava. Makes quiet interventions that slip ordinary behaviour into exhibition spaces.

## Who
Ondák works with small, almost unnoticed shifts: a queue that leads nowhere, a wall that slowly fills with pencil marks, a pavilion that is just the park continued indoors. He grew up in socialist Czechoslovakia, and many of his works carry a memory of that time (queuing, shortage, borders) while staying light and open. He represented the Czech and Slovak pavilion at the 2009 Venice Biennale. He matters because he shows how little needs to change for a space to become a work: one rule, carried out by ordinary people.

## Key works
- Good Feelings in Good Times, 2003. A group of people forms a queue in a public or museum space, waits a few minutes for nothing, then breaks up and forms again elsewhere. First shown at the Kölnischer Kunstverein, Cologne; shown at Frieze Art Fair in 2004, after which Tate acquired it. Performers are told not to act and, if asked, to say they are just queuing. [checked]
- It Will All Turn Out Right in the End, 2005 to 2006. A walk-in, scaled-down model of Tate Modern's Turbine Hall, built inside a small gallery of the same building (Tate Modern, 2006). [checked]
- Measuring the Universe, 2007. Gallery attendants mark each visitor's height on the white wall with their first name and the date. Over weeks thousands of marks merge into a dark band around the room. Shown at the Pinakothek der Moderne, Munich, 2007, at MoMA in 2009 and Tate St Ives in 2011. [checked]
- Loop, 2009. For the Czech and Slovak pavilion at the Venice Biennale, he continued the Giardini's own paths, trees and plants straight through the pavilion, so visitors walked "into" the building and found themselves still in the park. [checked]
- Time Capsule, 2011. An exact replica of the Fénix 2 capsule used in 2010 to rescue 33 miners trapped 700 metres underground in Chile for 69 days, hung in a darkened gallery from a rope and a shaft in the ceiling (Modern Art Oxford, 2011). [checked]
- Stampede, 2011. A large crowd rushed into and out of a gallery before the exhibition opened, leaving only traces. [checked]

## Core moves
- Staged ordinariness: performers who "do not act", using everyday props and behaviour.
- The empty queue: a social form with its goal removed.
- Accumulation: each visitor's trace adds to a shared image (the band of heights).
- Doubling the container: the building rebuilt at small scale inside itself.
- Continuation: the outside carried inside until the border disappears.
- Replica of a news object: a real rescue device remade as a silent, empty sculpture.

## Ideas worth keeping
Ondák trusts the viewer's habits more than the viewer's attention. People join a queue because a queue exists. People like being measured. A visitor keeps walking because the path keeps going. He builds works out of these reflexes, so that the meaning appears only afterwards, when you realise you were part of it, or that nothing was "there". The measuring piece turns a private family ritual (marking a child's height on a door frame) into a public portrait of everyone who came, a slow drawing made by strangers. In his own words at MoMA: "Every visitor [who] enters my room is welcome to be measured."

His works also hold history lightly. A queue in Cologne recalls Eastern European shortage; a capsule recalls being buried alive and pulled back into the world. The object is exact, but the feeling is left for the viewer to supply.

## What Mario could learn
- Take a social habit and remove its purpose; see what is left.
- Let the audience be the material, so the work grows with each visitor.
- Replicate a real object from the news in silence, without commentary, and let its emptiness speak.
- Blur inside and outside: carry the context into the frame.
- Do not reuse: a queue for nothing, height marks on a gallery wall, a model of the museum inside the museum, a park continued through a pavilion, a replica of the Chilean rescue capsule.
