# Douglas Huebler

American, 1924 to 1997. Worked in Massachusetts, New York and Los Angeles, where he taught at CalArts from 1976 to 1988. A founding figure of Conceptual art.

## Who
Huebler stopped making objects in 1968 and organised his work around time, place, or both. His pieces combine typed statements with photographs, maps and documents, often recording a system that he set in motion and then followed. He was one of the four artists in Seth Siegelaub's January 5 to 31, 1969 show. As a teacher at CalArts he influenced Mike Kelley and others.

## Key works
- Cross Manhattan Rectangle, 1968. A place-based piece shown in the January 5 to 31, 1969 exhibition, for which he wrote planning notes. [checked]
- Duration Piece #6, 1968. Typed text and thirteen photographs. It was shown during the first six hours of the January 1969 exhibition. [checked]
- Duration Piece #17 (Truro), 1968. A text panel with eight photographs, given to MOCA Los Angeles by Mike Kelley in Huebler's memory. [checked]
- Statement, 1969. A short text saying he no longer wished to add objects to the world and preferred to state the existence of things in terms of time and place. It appeared in the January 1969 catalogue context; exact first printing [verify]. [checked]
- Location Piece #2, New York City to Seattle, Washington, 1969 (published 1970). An envelope holding sixteen photographic prints, two photocopied maps and a page of commentary. [checked]
- Variable Piece #70 (In Process) Global, begun 1971. A project to document photographically, as completely as possible, the existence of everyone alive. The small set of actual images makes the impossible aim visible. [checked]

## Core moves
- Duration: a stretch of time becomes the shape of the work.
- Location: a route or set of points on a map is the work.
- System plus camera: a rule decides when and where to take the photograph.
- Documentation: text and images prove that something occurred.
- Impossibility: an aim that can never be met, pursued anyway.
- Withdrawal from objects: declining to add more things to the world.

## Ideas worth keeping
Huebler's photographs are not meant to be good pictures. They are evidence that a rule was followed: a picture every so many seconds, a mark at each point on a line. The typed statement tells you the rule, the images show the result, and the meaning lives in the fit or misfit between them. This is a quiet, almost bureaucratic way of making art about the world without making a new object.

His later variable pieces grow funny and sad at once. A plan to photograph everyone alive admits from the start that it will fail, and the pile of actual photos shows how little any record can hold. His short line on objects is the motto of the whole practice: "I do not wish to add any more" (MAMCO).

## What Mario could learn
- State a rule in one plain sentence, then let the documents show what the rule produced.
- Use time and place as the only materials: a duration, a route, a set of coordinates.
- Set an impossible goal and make the gap between goal and result the subject.
- Let ordinary paperwork (receipts, maps, typed notes) carry the work.
- Do not reuse: a project to photograph every living person; "the world is full of objects" and "I do not wish to add any more"; numbered Duration, Location and Variable pieces; photographs taken at fixed timed intervals as evidence; mailing items to towns along a line of latitude.
