# Rirkrit Tiravanija

Thai, born 1961 in Buenos Aires, the son of a Thai diplomat; raised in Thailand, Ethiopia and Canada. Divides his time between New York, Berlin and Chiang Mai. Known for turning galleries into kitchens and social spaces.

## Who
Since 1990 Tiravanija has made exhibitions in which he cooks and serves food, often Thai dishes, to whoever comes. The art is not the curry or the pots but the situation: people eating, talking and staying together in a space normally kept for looking. He became the best-known example of what the curator Nicolas Bourriaud called relational aesthetics in his 1998 book of that name: art whose material is human relations. His 2023 survey at MoMA PS1, A LOT OF PEOPLE, restaged many of these works.

## Key works
- untitled 1990 (pad thai), 1990. At the opening of his solo show at Paula Allen Gallery, New York, he cooked and served pad thai to visitors. One of the works he calls "situations". Restaged at MoMA PS1 in 2023. [checked]
- untitled 1992 (free), 1992. At his first solo show at 303 Gallery in SoHo, he moved the contents of the back office into the gallery, turned the office into a kitchen and gave away Thai curry to anyone. MoMA holds the work as untitled (free/still), dated 1992/1995/2007/2011, and can show it as leftovers or cook it again. [checked]
- untitled 1996 (tomorrow is another day), 1996. At the Kölnischer Kunstverein, Cologne, a full-size plywood copy of his East Village apartment, open around the clock for six weeks for visitors to live, cook and rest in. [checked]
- untitled 1998 (the land), from 1998. A shared plot near Chiang Mai, in Sanpatong, set up with Kamin Lertchaiprasert, where people farm and build. Ongoing and always changing. [checked]

## Core moves
- Gift or exchange: something is given away for free and the giving is the work.
- Hospitality as form: cooking, serving and eating together replace the object.
- Swap front and back: the hidden office or storage becomes the exhibit, the gallery becomes a kitchen.
- Leftovers as record: pans, tables and dirty dishes remain as the trace of the event.
- Open access: a space anyone can enter and use, at any hour.
- Title as date and label: "untitled", the year, and a plain word in brackets.

## Ideas worth keeping
Tiravanija's work asks what happens if art stops being something you look at and becomes something you take part in. A meal is a perfect case: it is shared, it is used up, and it leaves a mess that says more about the event than any photograph. By serving food in the gallery he also brings a specific culture, Thai cooking, into a Western white cube, and lets that culture set the terms.

The work is also a test of the institution. If the art is free food, can a museum own it? MoMA's answer is a list of materials and instructions, so the piece can be cooked again. He has said he hopes that through cooking and eating together "it is possible to cross physical and imaginary boundaries" (ArtAsiaPacific).

## What Mario could learn
- Imagine an artwork that is an event between people, and present only its recipe and its leftovers.
- Turn the hidden part of an institution (the office, the server, the training process) into the main room.
- Give something away and make the terms of the gift the text.
- Use a title system: year plus one bracketed word, and let the word carry the whole idea.
- Do not reuse: cooking pad thai or curry in a gallery, a gallery office turned into a kitchen, a copy of the artist's apartment open all night, the "untitled year (word)" title formula, a communal farm as artwork.
